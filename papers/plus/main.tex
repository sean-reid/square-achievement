\title{A short winning rule for the square achievement game}
\author{Sean Reid}
\date{September 2026}

\begin{document}

\begin{abstract}
Two players alternately claim cells of an $n \times n$ grid, and the
first to hold the four corners of an axis-aligned square wins. In
earlier work we gave a first-player strategy that wins on every board of
size at least $6$; it consulted a lookup table of some eight thousand
forcing plans. Here we give a strategy for the same boards that uses no
table. It is stated as a rule: open at $(2,2)$, answer the first reply
with a fixed second move, take a completion or a block whenever one is
available, and otherwise play the shortest line of at most three single
forcing moves followed by the arms of a \emph{plus}, a held stone with
the four cells at a common distance along the axes. The rule wins every
game by the seventeenth ply, plays the same $79$ lines on every board,
and reads only its own stones, the opponent's stones inside a $17 \times
17$ region, and one derived cell. Boards of size $6$ to $17$ are
verified by exhaustive forced-tree search, and a run on the $23 \times
23$ board is transported to every larger board by the simulation theorem
of the earlier work. The argument is formalized in Lean~4; the formal
theorem states that the tree of the rule's own moves is a winning tree,
not only that a winning tree exists.
\end{abstract}

\maketitle

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

Two players alternately claim empty cells of an $n \times n$ grid,
the first player moving first. A player wins upon holding all four
corners of a square with horizontal and vertical sides, of any size:
the cells $(r,c)$, $(r,c+s)$, $(r+s,c)$, $(r+s,c+s)$ for some
$s \ge 1$. A full board with no such square is a draw. Erickson posed
the game \cite{erickson-pearls, opg}; it is an achievement game in
the sense of Harary \cite{harary}, and the general theory is in Beck
\cite{beck}.

In \cite{reid-threshold} we proved that the first player has a
winning strategy if and only if $n \ge 5$. The draws up to $n = 4$
were reported from computer searches by Jenrich \cite{jenrich}; the
wins from $n = 15$ follow by strategy stealing from the theorem of
Bacher and Eliahou \cite{bacher-eliahou} that every two-coloring of
the $15 \times 15$ grid contains a monochromatic square; and for
$5 \le n \le 14$ nothing had been published until the concurrent
searches of Adler \cite{adler}, which report first-player wins within
$13$ plies for $6 \le n \le 14$, with certified strategy trees for
$n = 6$ and $7$. The strategy in
\cite{reid-threshold} for $n \ge 6$ consists of two reflexes, complete
a square if possible and otherwise block one, over a lookup table of
$237$ branches and $8{,}135$ forcing plans confined to the
$6 \times 6$ corner of the board. The table was produced by search,
and \cite{reid-threshold} left open whether a winning strategy for all
$n \ge 6$ can be stated without one.

We give such a strategy here. Its free moves are chosen by a rule
built on one shape, the \emph{plus}: a stone together with the four
cells at a common distance from it along the axes. Once the centre
and one arm are held, each further arm makes three corners of a square
whose fourth corner is a diagonal cell, so the opponent must block
there, and the last arm threatens two squares at once. The rule opens
at $(2,2)$, answers the first reply with a fixed second move, and then
follows a line of at most three single forcing moves, each of which
places a stone the opponent must answer, ending in the arms of a
plus. Section~\ref{sec:defs} defines the terms and
Section~\ref{sec:rule} states the rule in full.

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:main}
For every $n \ge 6$, the first player following the rule wins the
square achievement game on the $n \times n$ board.
\end{theorem}

No data enters the rule or the proof beyond seven exceptional second
moves; the Lean definition of the rule is $250$ lines, most of them
the search for the line. The theorem is about the rule's own play.
Its formal statement is
\[
  \texttt{plus\_wins\_with} \;:\; 6 \le n \;\longrightarrow\;
  \texttt{WinsWith}\ \texttt{plusMove}\ n\ [\,],
\]
where $\texttt{WinsWith}\ \sigma\ n\ h$ says that the game tree in
which the first player plays $\sigma$'s move at every node after the
history $h$, against every legal reply, ends in a first-player square
on every branch. The theorem of \cite{reid-threshold} states that a
winning tree exists, namely one the checker found; here the tree is
the one the rule plays. The rule also wins faster than the table: by
the seventeenth ply on every board, where the table strategy needed
$27$. On every board from $6$ to $17$ it plays the same $79$ lines,
each with at most six first-player moves after the opening pair.

The proof follows \cite{reid-threshold} with one addition. Boards of
size $6$ to $17$ are verified by a forced-tree checker that evaluates
the rule at every node. For larger boards a checked run on the $23
\times 23$ board, with three families of anchored runs, is transported
to every $n \ge 17$ by the simulation theorem of \cite{reid-threshold}.
The addition is an agreement lemma: the rule reads the same move from a
real history and from its image on the base board, in which far replies
are replaced by fixed stand-ins, because the stand-ins lie beyond the
region the rule looks at and share the latent corner with the stones
they replace. That lemma is what makes the simulation a statement about
the rule rather than about the existence of a win.

\section{Definitions}\label{sec:defs}

Cells are pairs $(r,c)$ of non-negative integers, row then column,
with row $0$ at the top and column $0$ at the left in the figures, and
$\Board_n = \{0,\dots,n-1\}^2$. The square anchored at $(r,c)$ with
side $s \ge 1$ is
$\Sq(r,c,s) = \{(r,c), (r,c+s), (r+s,c), (r+s,c+s)\}$. We call the
first player \emph{we} and the second player \emph{the opponent}. We
\emph{threaten} at a cell $z$ if $z$ is empty and $z$ together with
three of our stones is a square; the opponent \emph{blocks} by playing
$z$. A move that makes exactly one threat is \emph{forcing}; a move
that makes two threats wins, since one block leaves the other.
$\Board_6$ is the corner region in which the rule plays and
$\Board_{17}$ the base board whose stones it reads verbatim.
Table~\ref{tab:terms} collects the terms of this section.

\subsection{Pluses}

\begin{definition}[plus]\label{def:plus}
The \emph{plus} of side $s$ at the \emph{centre} $c = (r,k)$ has four
\emph{arms}, the cells $(r-s,k)$, $(r+s,k)$, $(r,k-s)$, $(r,k+s)$, and
four \emph{diagonals}, the cells $(r \pm s, k \pm s)$. Two arms are
\emph{perpendicular} if one is above or below the centre and the other
to its left or right; the diagonal between them is the cell in the row
of one and the column of the other. A plus lies in the region if its
arms and diagonals do, that is, $s \le r, k$ and $r+s, k+s \le 5$.
\end{definition}

The region holds $16$ pluses of side $1$, with centres in
$\{1,\dots,4\}^2$, and four of side $2$, with centres in
$\{2,3\}^2$; side $3$ does not fit.

\begin{lemma}[arms force]\label{lem:arms}
Suppose we hold the centre $c$ and one arm $a$ of a plus of side $s$,
and the opponent holds none of its arms and none of its diagonals.
Playing an arm $b$ perpendicular to $a$ threatens at the diagonal
between $a$ and $b$. If we also hold the arm $a'$ opposite to $a$,
playing $b$ threatens at two cells, the diagonals between $a$ and $b$
and between $a'$ and $b$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Say $a = (r-s,k)$ and $b = (r,k-s)$. Then $c$, $a$, $b$ and
$d = (r-s,k-s)$ are the corners of $\Sq(r-s,k-s,s)$, so with $c, a, b$
held and $d$ empty we threaten at $d$. With $a' = (r+s,k)$ also held,
$c$, $a'$, $b$ and $(r+s,k-s)$ are the corners of $\Sq(r,k-s,s)$, and
$(r+s,k-s) \ne d$. The other placements are symmetric.
\end{proof}

So from a centre and one arm, three moves win against an opponent who
has no threat and whose blocks make none: the two arms perpendicular
to the held arm each force a block on a diagonal, and the opposite arm
threatens at two diagonals at once. Blocks land on diagonals, never
on arms. Figure~\ref{fig:basic} plays this out. The opening $(2,2)$
and the second move $(3,2)$ are the centre and an arm of the side-$1$
plus at $(2,2)$, and an arm and the centre of the side-$1$ plus at
$(3,2)$, so most lines need no preparation.

\subsection{Tempo moves and lines}

When no plus can be completed directly, because the opponent holds an
arm or a diagonal or because our arms would not force, the rule first
plays moves that each force a block and so add a stone of ours.

\begin{definition}[tempo move]\label{def:tempo}
In a position where the opponent has no threat, a \emph{tempo move} is
a cell in $\Board_6$ that makes exactly one threat, such that after
the block the opponent still has no threat, and the block is not the
latent corner of Definition~\ref{def:latent}.
\end{definition}

A tempo move and its block add one stone to each side and return the
move to us; our stone is where we chose, theirs where we forced it.
Figure~\ref{fig:tempo} shows two tempo moves preparing a plus of side
$2$.

\begin{definition}[line, key]\label{def:line}
A \emph{line} from a position consists of some tempo moves, each
followed by its block, and then the arms of one plus in the region
that we do not yet hold, in some order, such that each arm before the
last makes exactly one threat whose block is not the latent corner,
the last arm makes at least two threats, and the opponent never has a
threat. The \emph{key} of a line is the triple
\[
  (\text{number of tempo moves},\ \text{number of arms to play},\
  \text{side of the plus}).
\]
The rule prefers the smallest key in lexicographic order, then the
smaller centre in cell order, then the line its search generates
first.
\end{definition}

The rule searches for lines with at most three tempo moves. Whether a
line exists, and which is preferred, depends only on the abstract
position defined next.

\subsection{Far stones and the latent corner}

\begin{definition}[latent corner, abstraction]\label{def:latent}
Let $x$ and $y$ be two cells each having a coordinate at least $12$.
Their \emph{latent corner} is a cell $b \in \Board_6$ such that
$b$, $x$, $y$ are three corners of one square. The \emph{abstraction}
of a position in which we have moved at least twice consists of our
stones, the opponent's stones inside $\Board_{17}$, and the latent
corner of the opponent's first two stones, if they have one.
\end{definition}

A pair has at most one latent corner. If $x$ and $y$ share a column,
the square has side $|x_1 - y_1|$ and $b$ lies in the row of the
member with row below $6$, at that distance to the left; a shared row
is symmetric; and if $x$ and $y$ are diagonally opposite, the two
remaining corners are $(x_1, y_2)$ and $(y_1, x_2)$, of which at most
one lies in $\Board_6$, since otherwise all four coordinates of $x$
and $y$ would be below $6$. The square joins a coordinate below $6$ to
one of at least $12$, so its side is at least $7$, and it often leaves
$\Board_{17}$; Figure~\ref{fig:latent} shows one that does.

Two facts from \cite[Section~6]{reid-threshold} explain why this cell
is recorded. First, two stones in $\Board_6$ lie in a common
square only if that square lies in $\Board_{12}$. In a position the
rule reaches, the opponent's stones are the two free replies and
forced blocks, and the blocks lie in $\Board_6$; so a stone beyond
$\Board_{17}$ shares no square with two blocks, and a square through
such a stone and one block has both free replies as its other
corners, with the block as their latent corner. Second, by the same
containment, every opponent square that leaves $\Board_{17}$ is of
this kind. Hence the stones the rule does not see can affect the game
only through the latent corner. The clause of
Definition~\ref{def:tempo} keeping blocks off that cell is what stops
a far pair from ever coming into play: were the opponent forced to
block on $b$ in Figure~\ref{fig:latent}, they would hold three corners
of the dashed square and threaten at $z$, and we would have to answer
instead of continuing our line. The rule applies the clause to every
pair with a latent corner, also when the square stays inside
$\Board_{17}$ and the opponent's stones are visible anyway; there it
costs nothing.

\section{The rule}\label{sec:rule}

Algorithm~\ref{alg:move} is the move function and
Algorithm~\ref{alg:line} the search it calls once, after the second
reply, to choose the line; both are stated as in the formal
development. The second move against a first reply $o_1$ is $(3,2)$
unless $o_1$ is one of seven cells, for which Figure~\ref{fig:second}
gives the move.

\begin{algorithm}[ht]
\caption{The move function.}\label{alg:move}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\If{some cell completes one of our squares}
  \State play the least such cell
\ElsIf{some cell completes an opponent square}
  \State play the least such cell
\ElsIf{we have not yet moved}
  \State play $(2,2)$
\ElsIf{we do not yet hold the second move for the first reply $o_1$}
  \State play it
\Else
  \State let $L$ be the line of Algorithm~\ref{alg:line} for the
    abstraction of the position after the second reply
  \State play the first cell of $L$ that we do not hold
\EndIf
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

Lines 1 to 4 scan the squares of the abstraction: ours, which all lie
in $\Board_6$, and the opponent's with all corners inside
$\Board_{17}$. An opponent square with a corner beyond $\Board_{17}$
would go through the latent corner, which the rule never surrenders,
and the checker of Section~\ref{sec:verify} confirms at every node
that the opponent has no threat at all.

\begin{algorithm}[ht]
\caption{The line search, from an abstract position $A$.}\label{alg:line}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\State $F \gets \{(A, \text{empty prefix})\}$
\For{$d = 0, 1, 2, 3$}
  \ForAll{$(B, p) \in F$ in order, if the opponent has no threat in $B$}
    \ForAll{pluses in the region whose centre we hold in $B$, side $1$ then $2$}
      \State the arms to play are those we do not hold; skip the plus if the opponent holds one
      \State try the orders of these arms; keep the first that wins by force (Definition~\ref{def:line})
    \EndFor
    \State record the best plus found from $B$, by (arms to play, side, centre), behind $p$
  \EndFor
  \If{some plus was recorded} \Return the recorded line with the smallest key \EndIf
  \State $F \gets \{(B', p\,m) : (B, p) \in F,\ m \text{ a tempo move in } B,\ B' = B \text{ after } m \text{ and its block}\}$
\EndFor
\State \Return no line
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

The loop over $d$ examines all lines with $d$ tempo moves before any
with $d+1$; within one $d$ the key compares arms to play, then side,
then centre. Tempo moves are tried in cell order and arm orders in a
fixed enumeration, so that two implementations choose the same line;
the Lean definition and the Python twin are compared on this.

The exceptions are exactly the first replies from which $(3,2)$ fails.
They were found by search: for each first reply on $\Board_6$ the
second move is the first cell in the order $(3,2)$, $(2,3)$, $(2,1)$,
$(1,2)$ against which the rest of the rule beats every second reply.
All seven lie within one king's move of the opening or of $(3,2)$; the
farthest, $(4,3)$, is a diagonal of the side-$1$ plus at $(3,2)$, the
cell on which the default line would need the opponent to block.

\subsection{Two games}

The line is chosen once, after the second reply, and followed. Since
the opponent's blocks are forced, every later position on a branch of
the verified tree is determined by the line, and our stones at any
point are the opening pair and a prefix of it. A rule that recomputed
the line at every move would also be a rule of this kind; the
committed version makes the verified tree a function of the reply
pair, which the argument of Section~\ref{sec:uniform} needs.

The compiled Lean definition finds the line from an abstract position
in about $80$ microseconds, the Python twin in a quarter of a
millisecond. The search has at most three
levels of tempo moves with at most $36$ candidates each, and at each
position at most $20$ pluses with at most $24$ arm orders.

\section{How the rule was found}\label{sec:how}

The table of \cite{reid-threshold} was generated by depth-first search
over forcing moves on $\Board_{15}$, with a preference for short plans.
It has $8{,}135$ plan entries and $248$ latent entries, $8{,}383$ in
all, but only $186$ distinct plans, and $8{,}354$ entries begin with
$(2,1)$ or $(2,3)$, an arm of the side-$1$ plus at $(2,2)$ or at
$(3,2)$. The shortest plans are that plus: two plans of three moves,
used in $157$ entries, play its three free arms and win on the ninth
ply. The longer plans, the bulk of the table, mostly continue from the
first arm into a ladder, a run of side-$1$ threats climbing a column
such as $(2,1)$, $(1,1)$, $(1,0)$, $(0,0)$, and finish elsewhere.
Replayed in the positions where they are used, the moves that make them
long do one of two things: they force a block so as to place a stone
that a later square needs, or they are the arms of a side-$2$ plus at
$(3,2)$, forced one at a time, the last making the double threat. This
suggested describing a plan as a plus, possibly prepared by a few
forcing moves.

A first attempt at a rule did not use shapes. It scored moves by the
number of threats we could make next and played the highest score. On
$\Board_6$ it leaves $31$ reply pairs without a win, all with both
replies near the opening, where the right move is a preparation whose
value a one-move lookahead does not see. The plus rule replaces the
score by a target: which plus can be completed by force, allowing a
few tempo moves first. The order of preference, fewer tempo moves and
then a smaller plus, keeps the lines short and makes the choice
unambiguous, which the formal development needs.

Two constants were settled by trying alternatives. Three tempo moves
are necessary, since with two, $25$ reply pairs on $\Board_6$ have no
line, and sufficient on every board up to $17$; a fourth is never
used. The base board of the abstraction was set at $17$ after $12$
failed: reading only the opponent's stones inside $\Board_{12}$ still
gives every reply pair a line, but fails the forced-tree check, since
a stone with a coordinate between $12$ and $16$ can complete a square
of side at least $7$ with one stone of the region and one near stone,
and a rule that does not see the stone does not block. The base $17$
is also the constant of the dichotomy in \cite{reid-threshold}, the
least board such that a square with a corner in the region that
leaves it has the two free replies as its other corners; the two uses
of the constant agree.

Compiled into a table of the old format, the rule gives $237$
branches with $2{,}330$ plan entries and $140$ latent entries, against
$8{,}135$ and $248$, and the $79$ distinct lines that
Section~\ref{sec:measure} counts directly; the compiled table and the
rule agree move for move on every branch.

\section{Verification}\label{sec:verify}

Fix a strategy $\sigma$ and a board size $n$. The \emph{forced tree}
of $\sigma$ on $\Board_n$ is the game tree in which we play $\sigma$'s
move, the opponent's replies range over all empty cells at the first
two levels, and below every later node only over the cells that block
a threat of the move just played. The checker of \cite{reid-threshold}
walks this tree under guards: past the second reply, every move of
$\sigma$ wins outright or leaves a threat, every non-winning move and
every threat cell lies in $\Board_6$, and the opponent never has a
threat. Its soundness theorem said that if every branch ends in a
first-player square then the first player can win on $\Board_n$: a
reply that blocks nothing leaves a threat standing, which the strategy
completes next move, so every omitted reply loses in two plies. Here
the same checker is evaluated on the rule, and its soundness theorem
is stronger.

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:forced}
Let $\sigma$ be a strategy that, whenever our stones lie in $\Board_6$
and some cell completes one of our squares, plays such a cell. If the
forced tree of $\sigma$ on $\Board_n$ passes the guards and every
branch ends in a first-player square, then
$\texttt{WinsWith}\ \sigma\ n\ [\,]$.
\end{proposition}

The hypothesis is what the two-ply argument needs: if the winning
tree is to be $\sigma$'s own, $\sigma$ must be the one to complete the
standing threat. The rule satisfies it by its first clause, once the
executable scan it uses is known to agree with the checker's; that is
the file \texttt{Plus/Threats.lean} and the theorem
\texttt{plusMove\_takesWins}. The proposition is
\texttt{checkForcedS\_winsWith}. The table strategy of
\cite{reid-threshold} satisfies the hypothesis too, so the same
strengthening is available for it.

The rule passes the forced tree on every board from $6$ to $17$;
Table~\ref{tab:runs} gives the number of reply pairs at the root and
the time of the compiled checker. As in \cite{reid-threshold} the
search exists three times, a Python twin used while finding the rule,
the Lean definition, and the compiled Lean checker the proof cites.
The twin and the Lean rule are compared line for line on sample
positions in the test suite, and the Python verifier, which walks the
full tree rather than the forced skeleton, confirms the win on
$\Board_6$ with the same longest game.

\section{Every larger board}\label{sec:uniform}

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:uniform}
For every $n \ge 17$ the first player following the rule wins on
$\Board_n$.
\end{theorem}

The proof transports a checked run to every larger board, as in
\cite[Section~6]{reid-threshold}. In a position the forced run can
reach, the opponent's lasting stones are the two free replies and
forced blocks in $\Board_6$. Replies inside $\Board_{17}$ are read
verbatim; a reply beyond it is replaced, for the base run, by a
stand-in, a fixed cell beyond $\Board_{17}$ when the pair has no latent
corner and an anchor cell with the same latent corner when it has one.
The base run is on $\Board_{23}$, the least board holding every
anchor. Safety at every node reduces to the dichotomy of
Section~\ref{sec:defs}: an opponent square is either inside
$\Board_{17}$, and excluded by the base run, or goes through the
latent corner, which the rule never surrenders. Pairs with a latent
corner are covered by three families of anchored runs, one per
placement of the pair on the corners of its square, over all
coordinates: $176$ positions, each a run of the checker with the
latent corner as an additional guard.

The formal simulation theorem takes $\sigma$ as a parameter, concludes
$\texttt{WinsWith}\ \sigma\ n\ h$ for the real history $h$, and has
two hypotheses the table version did not need: that $\sigma$ takes its
completions, as in Proposition~\ref{prop:forced}, and agreement,
\[
  \sigma\,(\text{real history}) \;=\; \sigma\,(\text{base history})
\]
at every node, the base history being the real one with the two
replies replaced by their stand-ins. For the rule this follows from
what it reads. Stand-ins and anchors lie beyond $\Board_{17}$, as do
the stones they replace, so both drop out of the abstraction; a reply
inside $\Board_{17}$ is its own stand-in; and the stand-ins have the
same latent corner as the real pair, which for the anchors is part of
what the anchored run checks and for the keyless stand-ins is a short
computation. So the abstractions agree, and the rule's move is a
function of the abstraction and of the first reply's second move,
which agrees too because every reply beyond $\Board_6$ has the default
second move. This is \texttt{plusMove\_agree} in
\texttt{Plus/Agreement.lean}. The table strategy needed no such lemma
because its lookups were designed around the stand-ins; the rule needs
it because nothing in its definition mentions them.

\begin{remark}
The base board $23$, the stand-ins $(22,22)$ and $(22,21)$, and the
anchor cells at coordinates $17$ and above are choices of the proof,
not of the rule; any board of size at least $17$ holding the anchors
would do. The rule has two board constants, the region $6$ and the
base $17$, with $12 = 2 \cdot 6$ marking the stones that can have a
latent corner, and one search constant, the three tempo moves.
\end{remark}

Theorem~\ref{thm:main} follows from Table~\ref{tab:runs} for
$6 \le n \le 17$ and Theorem~\ref{thm:uniform} for $n \ge 17$. The
formal assembly is \texttt{plus\_wins\_with}; \texttt{plus\_wins} is
the corollary in the form of \cite{reid-threshold}, a first-player win
from the empty board with the moves forgotten.

\section{Formalization}\label{sec:lean}

The development of \cite{reid-threshold} defines the game and the
predicate \texttt{P1CanWin}: the first player can win from a position
if some legal move completes one of their squares, or some legal move
admits a reply and every reply completes nothing and leads to a
position from which the first player can win. A derivation is a
strategy tree. Two additions carry this paper.

The first is \texttt{WinsWith}. For a strategy $\sigma$, a board size
$n$, and a history $h$ with the first player to move,
$\texttt{WinsWith}\ \sigma\ n\ h$ holds if $\sigma$'s move at $h$ is
legal and completes a square, or is legal, admits a reply, and every
reply completes nothing and leads to a history at which
\texttt{WinsWith} holds again. It has the constructors of
\texttt{P1CanWin} with one extra premise on each, that the move is the
one $\sigma$ returns, and implies \texttt{P1CanWin} by forgetting that
premise. A checker that searched for a win and found one proves
\texttt{P1CanWin}; a checker that plays $\sigma$ at every node proves
\texttt{WinsWith} only if the soundness proof never invents a move,
and the one place it did, the two-ply win against a reply that blocks
nothing, is where Proposition~\ref{prop:forced} needs its hypothesis.

The second is the rule as a Lean definition, \texttt{plusMove}, with
the type of the table strategy: a function from the squares of the
board and the history to a move. The checker and the simulation
theorem were already generic in the strategy, so the rule is passed
where the table was. The library \texttt{Plus} has six files:
\texttt{Rule.lean}, the rule ($250$ lines); \texttt{Threats.lean}, its
threat scan agrees with the checker's ($190$); \texttt{Agreement.lean},
the agreement lemma ($110$); \texttt{Anchors.lean}, the anchored runs
($65$); \texttt{Uniform.lean}, the case analysis over reply pairs
assembling Theorem~\ref{thm:uniform} ($530$); \texttt{Results.lean},
the certificates and theorems ($70$). No table appears anywhere.

The certificates are discharged by native evaluation, as in
\cite{reid-threshold}: one for sizes $6$ to $16$, one for $17$, one
for the anchor board, and one per anchored family. The axioms of
\texttt{plus\_wins\_with} are \texttt{propext},
\texttt{Classical.choice}, \texttt{Quot.sound}, and these six
evaluation axioms; the kernel checks every proof except the
evaluations, which the Lean compiler and runtime perform. The library
builds in about thirteen minutes on a laptop, almost all of it the
evaluations.

\section{Measurements}\label{sec:measure}

For each board from $6$ to $17$ we ran the Python twin over every
reply pair and recorded the line, its tempo moves, and the ply of the
win against an opponent who blocks. Table~\ref{tab:lines} summarizes
the result. The set of lines does not depend
on the board: every board plays exactly the $79$ lines of $\Board_6$,
since a pair's line depends only on its abstraction. Most pairs need
no preparation: on $\Board_{17}$, $81{,}186$ of $82{,}368$ pairs are
won by a side-$1$ plus with no tempo move, the other $1{,}182$ by a
side-$2$ plus behind one to three tempo moves, and only $79$ pairs on
any board, all with both replies in $\Board_6$, need two or three.
And the rule wins by ply $17$ against $27$ for the table of
\cite{reid-threshold}, in the same measure, the ply of the winning
move against an opponent who always blocks. Adler's searches
\cite{adler} report wins by ply $13$ for $6 \le n \le 14$, so the rule
is not optimal in length.

\section{Reproducibility}\label{sec:repro}

The supplementary material contains the Lean development. In its
namespace \texttt{Plus}, Theorem~\ref{thm:main} is
\texttt{plus\_wins\_with} and Theorem~\ref{thm:uniform} is
\texttt{plus\_wins\_with\_of\_seventeen\_le};
Proposition~\ref{prop:forced} is \texttt{checkForcedS\_winsWith}. The
build evaluates every certificate, and two commands of the checker
executable reproduce the runs of Table~\ref{tab:runs}:
\begin{quote}
\texttt{lake exe checker $n$ plus} \\
\texttt{lake exe checker plus anchors}
\end{quote}
The Python twin lives in the pipeline as \texttt{squares/plus}:
\begin{quote}
\texttt{python -m squares.plus.sweep $n$} \\
\texttt{python -m squares.plus.measure}
\end{quote}
The first sweeps every reply pair on $\Board_n$ and replays the
anchored positions; the second produces Table~\ref{tab:lines}. The
test suite checks the twin against the Lean rule and runs the full
verifier on $\Board_6$.

\section{Open questions}\label{sec:open}

The rule wins by ply $17$; Adler's searches \cite{adler} find wins by
ply $13$ on each board from $6$ to $14$, one search per board. Is
there a rule of comparable length that wins by ply $13$ on every
board, and what is the least ply by which the first player can force
a win on $\Board_n$? Other orderings, or other target shapes, might
shorten the longest games at the cost of a longer statement.

The second move has seven exceptions. Is there an opening, or an
opening with a fixed second move, from which a rule of this kind wins
with none? The search that fixed the second move tried the four
neighbours of the opening in one order and did not vary the opening.

The rule does not apply to $\Board_5$, where from twenty reply pairs
no purely forcing continuation wins and the table of
\cite{reid-threshold} needed quiet moves with a case split on the
reply. A short rule for $\Board_5$ is open.

Finally, the constants. The region $6$ cannot shrink for a purely
forcing strategy from $(2,2)$ with the second moves searched in
\cite{reid-threshold}. The base $17$ is least for the dichotomy of
latent squares, and the proof needs an abstraction that reads
$\Board_{17}$ verbatim; whether a rule can read less, for instance
only the opponent's stones inside $\Board_6$ with a summary of the
rest, is open.
