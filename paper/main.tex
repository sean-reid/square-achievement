\title{The square achievement game on \texorpdfstring{$n \times n$}{n x n} boards}
\author{Sean Reid}
\date{September 2026}

\begin{document}

\begin{abstract}
Two players alternately claim cells of an $n \times n$ grid; the first
to hold all four corners of an axis-aligned square wins, and a full
board without one is a draw. We prove that the first player has a
winning strategy if and only if $n \ge 5$, every smaller board being
drawn under optimal play, which answers a question of Erickson. The
winning strategy for boards of size at least $6$ is stated as a rule
with no lookup table: open at $(2,2)$, answer the first reply with a
fixed second move, take a completion or a block whenever one is
available, and otherwise play the shortest line of at most three single
forcing moves followed by the arms of a \emph{plus}, a held stone with
the four cells at a common distance along the axes. The rule wins every
game by the seventeenth ply, plays the same $\distinctLines$ lines on
every board, and reads only its own stones, the opponent's stones inside
a $17 \times 17$ region, and one derived cell. Boards of size $5$ to $17$
are checked by machine, and a simulation theorem carries a
checked run on the $23 \times 23$ board to every larger board. The
argument is formalized in Lean~4, and the formal theorem for the rule
states that the tree of its own moves is a winning tree, not only that
a winning tree exists.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

Two players alternately claim empty cells of an $n \times n$ grid, the
first player moving first. A player wins upon holding all four corners
of a square with horizontal and vertical sides, of any size: the cells
$(r,c)$, $(r,c+s)$, $(r+s,c)$, $(r+s,c+s)$ for some $s \ge 1$. If the
board fills with no player holding such a square, the game is a draw.

Erickson posed the game and asked for the outcome under optimal play
\cite{erickson-pearls, opg}. It is an achievement game in the sense of
Harary \cite{harary}, played in the Maker--Maker convention: both
players pursue the same goal, and a player who loses the race may still
hold out for a draw. The convention goes back to the positional games
of Hales and Jewett \cite{hales-jewett}; a recent unified treatment of
achievement conventions, with the strategy-stealing theory in the
generality we use, is \cite{galliot-senizergues}, and the general
theory of positional games is in Beck \cite{beck}.

Two boundary results were known. Jenrich \cite{jenrich} reported
computer searches showing that the second player can secure a draw for
$n = 3, 4$ and that the first player can force a win for $n = 5$.
Bacher and Eliahou \cite{bacher-eliahou} proved that every two-coloring
of the $15 \times 15$ grid contains four equal-colored cells at the
corners of an axis-aligned square, and that $15$ is least with this
property. Since no completed game on a board of size $15$ or more is
square-free, strategy stealing gives the first player a win there.
Between these results nothing was published: for $6 \le n \le 14$
neither a winning strategy nor a drawing strategy was known
\cite{opg}, and no coloring argument can reach this range, since Bacher
and Eliahou also exhibit square-free two-colorings of the $14 \times 14$
board. Concurrently with the present work, Adler \cite{adler} announced
computer searches settling these nine sizes as first-player wins, with
full strategy trees certified for $n = 6$ and $7$ and search verdicts
for the rest, one search per size; the author notes that the results
are unrefereed and names a conceptual proof with an explicit strategy
as the open direction.

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:main}
In the square achievement game on the $n \times n$ board, the first
player has a winning strategy if and only if $n \ge 5$. For $n \le 4$
the game is drawn under optimal play: each player can secure at least a
draw.
\end{theorem}

The strategy that wins for $n \ge 6$ is a rule built around one shape,
the \emph{plus}: a stone together with the four cells at a common
distance from it along the axes. Its arms force the opponent's replies
one at a time, and the last arm threatens two squares at once.
Section~\ref{sec:defs} defines the terms and Section~\ref{sec:rule}
states the rule.

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:rule}
For every $n \ge 6$, the first player following the plus rule wins the
square achievement game on the $n \times n$ board, by the
$\latestPly$th ply at the latest.
\end{theorem}

\subsection*{Contributions}
\begin{enumerate}
\item \emph{The outcome} (Theorem~\ref{thm:main}). The first player
  wins exactly on the boards of size at least $5$, and the smaller
  boards are drawn in the strong sense that each player can secure a
  draw. This answers Erickson's question.
\item \emph{A rule that wins} (Theorem~\ref{thm:rule},
  Section~\ref{sec:rule}). One explicit strategy, stated in a paragraph
  and defined in $\ruleLeanLines$ lines of Lean with no data beyond
  seven exceptional second moves, wins every board from $6 \times 6$ up.
  It plays the same $\distinctLines$ lines on every board and wins by
  the $\latestPly$th ply.
\item \emph{Every board at once} (Theorem~\ref{thm:uniform}). A
  simulation theorem, stated for any strategy, transports a checked run
  on the $23 \times 23$ board to every larger board. Its hypotheses are
  finitely many decidable properties of the strategy, each discharged
  by evaluation, so the range $n \ge 15$, previously reached only by
  strategy stealing from the theorem of Bacher and Eliahou, is covered
  by the explicit strategy.
\item \emph{A machine-checked proof} (Section~\ref{sec:lean}). The
  game, the checkers with their soundness theorems, strategy stealing,
  determinacy, the simulation theorem, and the rule are formalized in
  Lean~4, so Theorems~\ref{thm:main} and~\ref{thm:rule} are
  kernel-checked declarations, the latter in the form
  $\texttt{plus\_wins\_with} : 6 \le n \to
  \texttt{WinsWith}\ \texttt{plusMove}\ n\ [\,]$: the tree in which the
  first player plays the rule's move at every node is winning, not
  merely some tree the checker found.
\item \emph{Measurements} (Section~\ref{sec:measure}). The lines the
  rule plays, their lengths, and the cost of every certificate,
  produced by the accompanying code.
\end{enumerate}

Theorem~\ref{thm:main} is proved in three ranges: an exhaustive
refutation for $n \le 4$ (Section~\ref{sec:small}), a strategy table
checked by machine for $n = 5$, where no purely forcing strategy from
the opening $(2,2)$ exists (Section~\ref{sec:small}), and the rule for $n \ge 6$,
checked at every size up to $17$ (Section~\ref{sec:verify}) and carried
to every larger board by the simulation theorem
(Section~\ref{sec:uniform}). Searches are only as trustworthy as the
programs running them, and this game is a case in point: the first
version of the search in \cite{jenrich} contained an error and was
corrected later. The formalization in Lean~4 \cite{lean4}, on mathlib
\cite{mathlib}, makes every search discharge a statement about the
abstract game rather than about a program.

\section{Definitions}\label{sec:defs}

Cells are pairs $(r,c)$ of non-negative integers, row then column,
with row $0$ at the top and column $0$ at the left in the figures, and
$\Board_n = \{0,\dots,n-1\}^2$. For $s \ge 1$ the square anchored at
$(r,c)$ with side $s$ is
$\Sq(r,c,s) = \{(r,c), (r,c+s), (r+s,c), (r+s,c+s)\}$; a set of cells
contains a square if it contains some $\Sq(r,c,s)$. The case $s = 1$ is
allowed, so the smallest winning shape is a $2 \times 2$ block, the
convention of \cite{jenrich, bacher-eliahou}; tilted squares do not
count. A \emph{position} is a pair of disjoint subsets of $\Board_n$,
the stones of the two players. The game is finite with perfect
information, so exactly one of three holds: the first player can force
a win, the second can, or both can force at least a draw.

We call the first player \emph{we} and the second player \emph{the
opponent}. We \emph{threaten} at a cell $z$ if $z$ is empty and $z$
together with three of our stones is a square; the opponent
\emph{blocks} by playing $z$. A move that makes exactly one threat is
\emph{forcing}; a move that makes two threats wins, since one block
leaves the other. $\Board_6$ is the corner region in which the rule
plays and $\Board_{17}$ the base board whose stones it reads verbatim.
Table~\ref{tab:terms} collects the terms of this section.

\subsection{Pluses}

\begin{definition}[plus]\label{def:plus}
The \emph{plus} of side $s$ at the \emph{centre} $c = (r,k)$ has four
\emph{arms}, the cells $(r-s,k)$, $(r+s,k)$, $(r,k-s)$, $(r,k+s)$, and
four \emph{diagonals}, the cells $(r \pm s, k \pm s)$. Two arms are
\emph{perpendicular} if one is above or below the centre and the other
to its left or right; the diagonal between them is the cell in the row
of one and the column of the other. A plus lies in the region if its
arms and diagonals do, that is, $s \le r, k$ and $r+s, k+s \le 5$.
\end{definition}

The region holds $16$ pluses of side $1$, with centres in
$\{1,\dots,4\}^2$, and four of side $2$, with centres in $\{2,3\}^2$;
side $3$ does not fit.

\begin{lemma}[arms force]\label{lem:arms}
Suppose we hold the centre $c$ and one arm $a$ of a plus of side $s$,
and the opponent holds none of its arms and none of its diagonals.
Playing an arm $b$ perpendicular to $a$ threatens at the diagonal
between $a$ and $b$. If we also hold the arm $a'$ opposite to $a$,
playing $b$ threatens at two cells, the diagonals between $a$ and $b$
and between $a'$ and $b$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Say $a = (r-s,k)$ and $b = (r,k-s)$. Then $c$, $a$, $b$ and
$d = (r-s,k-s)$ are the corners of $\Sq(r-s,k-s,s)$, so with $c, a, b$
held and $d$ empty we threaten at $d$. With $a' = (r+s,k)$ also held,
$c$, $a'$, $b$ and $(r+s,k-s)$ are the corners of $\Sq(r,k-s,s)$, and
$(r+s,k-s) \ne d$. The other placements are symmetric.
\end{proof}

So from a centre and one arm, three moves win against an opponent who
has no threat and whose blocks make none: the two arms perpendicular to
the held arm each force a block on a diagonal, and the opposite arm
threatens at two diagonals at once. Blocks land on diagonals, never on
arms. Figure~\ref{fig:basic} plays this out. The opening $(2,2)$ and
the second move $(3,2)$ are the centre and an arm of the side-$1$ plus
at $(2,2)$, and an arm and the centre of the side-$1$ plus at $(3,2)$,
so most lines need no preparation.

\subsection{Tempo moves and lines}

When no plus can be completed directly, because the opponent holds an
arm or a diagonal or because our arms would not force, the rule first
plays moves that each force a block and so add a stone of ours.

\begin{definition}[tempo move]\label{def:tempo}
In a position where the opponent has no threat, a \emph{tempo move} is
a cell in $\Board_6$ that makes exactly one threat, such that after the
block the opponent still has no threat, and the block is not the hidden
corner of Definition~\ref{def:latent}.
\end{definition}

A tempo move and its block add one stone to each side and return the
move to us; our stone is where we chose, theirs where we forced it.
Figure~\ref{fig:tempo} shows two tempo moves preparing a plus of side
$2$.

\begin{definition}[line, key]\label{def:line}
A \emph{line} from a position consists of some tempo moves, each
followed by its block, and then the arms we do not yet hold of one plus
in the region whose centre we hold, in some order, such that each arm before the last
makes exactly one threat whose block is not the hidden corner, the last
arm makes at least two threats, and the opponent never has a threat.
The \emph{key} of a line is the triple
\[
  (\text{number of tempo moves},\ \text{number of arms to play},\
  \text{side of the plus}).
\]
The rule prefers the smallest key in lexicographic order, then the
smaller centre in cell order, then the line its search generates first.
\end{definition}

The rule searches for lines with at most three tempo moves. Whether a
line exists, and which is preferred, depends only on the abstract
position defined next.

\subsection{Far stones and the hidden corner}

The rule's stones never leave $\Board_6$, and the opponent's stones in
a position the rule reaches are two free replies and forced blocks,
the blocks in $\Board_6$ as well. Two containments say how far a
square through such stones can reach.

\begin{lemma}[span]\label{lem:span}
Let $x \ne y$ be distinct corners of a square $\Sq(r,c,s)$ with
$x, y \in \Board_B$. Then $\Sq(r,c,s) \subseteq \Board_{2B}$, and every
corner of $\Sq(r,c,s)$ has its row or its column indexed below $B$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Two distinct corners either share a coordinate and differ by $s$ in the
other, or differ by $s$ in both; either way $s < B$. Each anchor
coordinate is bounded by the corresponding coordinate of a corner, so
$r, c < B$, and the containment follows. If $x$ and $y$ share a row,
the two remaining corners inherit their columns, both below $B$; a
shared column is symmetric; and if $x$ and $y$ are diagonally opposite,
all four corners lie in $\Board_B$.
\end{proof}

With $B = 6$: two stones of the region lie in a common square only if
that square lies in $\Board_{12}$. Hence an opponent stone beyond
$\Board_{17}$, which the rule does not read, shares no square with two
forced blocks, and an opponent square through such a stone and a block
has the two free replies as its other corners. The next definition
names the block in that situation; it is the one cell through which
the stones the rule does not see can affect the game.

\begin{definition}[hidden corner, abstraction]\label{def:latent}
Let $x$ and $y$ be two cells each having a coordinate at least $12$.
Their \emph{hidden corner} is a cell $b \in \Board_6$ such that
$b$, $x$, $y$ are three corners of one square. The \emph{abstraction}
of a position reached after the opponent's second move consists of our
stones, the opponent's stones inside $\Board_{17}$, and the hidden
corner of the opponent's first two stones, if they have one.
\end{definition}

\begin{lemma}[dichotomy]\label{lem:dichotomy}
Two cells $x \ne y$ have at most one hidden corner. If $\Sq(r,c,s)$ has a corner
$b \in \Board_6$, has $x$ and $y$ among its other corners, and is not
contained in $\Board_{17}$, then $b = (r,c)$, $s \ge 12$, each of $x$,
$y$ has a coordinate at least $12$, and $b$ is the hidden corner of
$\{x, y\}$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Uniqueness: if $x$ and $y$ share a column, the square has side
$s = |x_1 - y_1|$ and $b$ lies in the row of a member with row below
$6$, at distance $s$ to the left, since the cell at that distance to
the right would put both coordinates of that member below $6$; only
one member can have row below $6$, since if both did then $s < 6$ and
the shared column, being the coordinate at least $12$ of either, would
put both candidates for $b$ at column $7$ or more; a shared row
is symmetric; if $x$ and $y$ are diagonally opposite, the two remaining
corners are $(x_1, y_2)$ and $(y_1, x_2)$, of which at most one lies in
$\Board_6$, since otherwise all four coordinates of $x$ and $y$ would
be below $6$. For the second claim, a second corner in $\Board_6$ would
put the square inside $\Board_{12}$ by Lemma~\ref{lem:span}, against
the hypothesis; so $b$ is alone in the region and, being the corner
nearest the origin, is $(r,c)$; the corner opposite $b$ has both
coordinates at most $5 + s$, and the square leaves $\Board_{17}$ only
if a coordinate reaches $17$, so $s \ge 12$. Each of $x$, $y$ has a
coordinate equal to $r + s$ or $c + s$, hence at least $12$. The formal
statement is in \texttt{General/Coverage.lean}.
\end{proof}

The clause of Definition~\ref{def:tempo} keeping blocks off the hidden
corner is what stops a far pair from ever coming into play: were the
opponent forced to block on $b$ in Figure~\ref{fig:latent}, they would
hold three corners of the dashed square and threaten at $z$, and we
would have to answer instead of continuing our line. The rule applies
the clause whenever the pair has a hidden corner, also when the square
stays inside $\Board_{17}$; there it costs nothing.

\begin{remark}
The base $17 = 6 + 2 \cdot 6 - 1$ is least for Lemma~\ref{lem:dichotomy}.
With base $15$ or $16$, the replies $(0,16)$ and $(11,5)$ span a square
through the region cell $(0,5)$ completing at $(11,16)$: it fits no
board of size $15$ or $16$, and $(11,5)$ has no coordinate at least
$12$.
\end{remark}

\section{The rule}\label{sec:rule}

Algorithm~\ref{alg:move} is the move function and
Algorithm~\ref{alg:line} the search it calls once, after the second
reply, to choose the line; both are stated as in the formal
development. The second move against a first reply $o_1$ is $(3,2)$
unless $o_1$ is one of seven cells, for which Figure~\ref{fig:second}
gives the move.

\begin{algorithm}[ht]
\caption{The move function.}\label{alg:move}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\If{some cell completes one of our squares}
  \State play the least such cell
\ElsIf{some cell completes an opponent square}
  \State play the least such cell
\ElsIf{we have not yet moved}
  \State play $(2,2)$
\ElsIf{we do not yet hold the second move for the first reply $o_1$}
  \State play it
\Else
  \State let $L$ be the line of Algorithm~\ref{alg:line} for the
    abstraction of the position after the second reply
  \State play the first cell of $L$ that we do not hold
\EndIf
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

Lines 1 to 4 scan the squares of the abstraction: ours, which all lie
in $\Board_6$, and the opponent's with all corners inside $\Board_{17}$.
An opponent square with a corner beyond $\Board_{17}$ would go through
the hidden corner, which the rule never surrenders, and the checker of
Section~\ref{sec:verify} confirms at every node that the opponent has
no threat at all.

\begin{algorithm}[ht]
\caption{The line search, from an abstract position $A$.}\label{alg:line}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\State $F \gets \{(A, \text{empty prefix})\}$
\For{$d = 0, 1, 2, 3$}
  \ForAll{$(B, p) \in F$ in order, if the opponent has no threat in $B$}
    \ForAll{pluses in the region whose centre we hold in $B$, side $1$ then $2$}
      \State the arms to play are those we do not hold; skip the plus if the opponent holds one
      \State try the orders of these arms; keep the first that wins by force (Definition~\ref{def:line})
    \EndFor
    \State record the best plus found from $B$, by (arms to play, side, centre), behind $p$
  \EndFor
  \If{some plus was recorded} \Return the recorded line with the smallest key \EndIf
  \State $F \gets \{(B', p\,m) : (B, p) \in F,\ m \text{ a tempo move in } B,\ B' = B \text{ after } m \text{ and its block}\}$
\EndFor
\State \Return no line
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

The loop over $d$ examines all lines with $d$ tempo moves before any
with $d+1$; within one $d$ the key compares arms to play, then side,
then centre. Tempo moves are tried in cell order and arm orders in a
fixed enumeration, so that two implementations choose the same line;
the Lean definition and the Python twin are compared on this.

The exceptions are exactly the first replies from which $(3,2)$ fails.
They were found by search: for each first reply on $\Board_6$ the
second move is the first cell in the order $(3,2)$, $(2,3)$, $(2,1)$,
$(1,2)$ against which the rest of the rule beats every second reply.
All seven lie within one king's move of the opening or of $(3,2)$; the
farthest, $(4,3)$, is a diagonal of the side-$1$ plus at $(3,2)$, the
cell on which the default line would need the opponent to block.

The line is chosen once, after the second reply, and followed. Since
the opponent's blocks are forced, every later position on a branch of
the verified tree is determined by the line, and our stones at any
point are the opening pair and a prefix of it. A rule that recomputed
the line at every move would also be a rule of this kind; the committed
version makes the verified tree a function of the reply pair, which the
argument of Section~\ref{sec:uniform} needs. The compiled Lean
definition finds the line from an abstract position in about $80$
microseconds, the Python twin in a quarter of a millisecond. The search
has at most three levels of tempo moves with at most $36$ candidates
each, and at each position at most $20$ pluses with at most $24$ arm
orders.

\section{Small boards and the \texorpdfstring{$5 \times 5$}{5 x 5} board}\label{sec:small}

\begin{lemma}[strategy stealing]\label{lem:stealing}
The second player has no winning strategy.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Suppose the second player had a winning strategy $\tau$. Let the first
player open with an arbitrary move and thereafter follow $\tau$ as if
the extra stone were absent, making a further arbitrary move whenever
$\tau$ selects an occupied cell. The winning condition is monotone in a
player's stone set, so surplus stones never hurt, and the first player
wins; but $\tau$ was assumed to win against all first-player play, a
contradiction. The formal statement is
\texttt{second\_player\_no\_win}; the monotonicity step is the lemma
\texttt{P1CanWin.insert\_left}, whose one subtlety is that the surplus
stone may itself complete a square, ending the game early.
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:small}
For $n \le 4$ the first player has no winning strategy. With
Lemma~\ref{lem:stealing}, these games are drawn under optimal play:
each player can secure at least a draw.
\end{proposition}

For $n \le 2$ the board has at most four cells, so neither player ever
holds four. For $n = 3, 4$ the claim is a
statement about a finite game tree, verified by an exhaustive search
that is part of the formal development: the search shows that the
player to move cannot force a win by exhibiting, for every first-player
move, a reply after which the first player still cannot force one. Two
standard reductions keep the tree small: a player able to complete a
square wins immediately, and a player facing two distinct completion
threats has lost. The $4 \times 4$ search runs in about ninety seconds
compiled. This confirms the report of \cite{jenrich}. The step from
``neither player can force a win'' to ``each player can secure a draw''
is determinacy of the finite game, formalized as
\texttt{P1CanWin.or\_p2CanDraw} by induction on the number of empty
cells; the drawing strategies of both players on each board are the
theorem \texttt{small\_board\_drawn}.

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:five}
The first player has a winning strategy on $\Board_5$.
\end{proposition}

The $5 \times 5$ board is won, but not by any purely forcing strategy
from the opening $(2,2)$: for $\fiveNoForcing$ of the reply pairs that
arise, no continuation in which every first-player move makes a threat
wins, a fact established by exhaustive analysis. The strategy for this
board is therefore a table with \emph{branch points}: a quiet,
non-threatening move followed by a sub-table indexed by the reply to
it. It was
extracted from a full solution of the $5 \times 5$ game,
$\fiveTablePositions$ positions solved with draws scored against the
first player, has $\fiveTableBranches$ branches, and is verified by a
checker that walks the complete game tree, every reply at every node.
It plays the same two reflexes as the rule, complete a square when
possible and otherwise block one, before consulting the table. Its
verification and soundness theorem are in Section~\ref{sec:verify}; the
table itself is data in the formal development and is not otherwise
described here.

\section{Verification}\label{sec:verify}

Fix a strategy $\sigma$ and a board size $n$. The \emph{verification
tree} of $\sigma$ on $\Board_n$ is the game tree in which the first
player's moves are given by $\sigma$ and the second player's moves
range over all empty cells; $\sigma$ \emph{verifies} on $\Board_n$ if
every branch ends in a first-player square. If $\sigma$ verifies then
the first player wins, and the $5 \times 5$ table is checked this way,
its checker walking the tree in full.

The rule is verified on a smaller tree. The \emph{forced tree} of
$\sigma$ on $\Board_n$ is the game tree in which we play $\sigma$'s
move, the opponent's replies range over all empty cells at the first
two levels, and below every later node only over the cells that block
a threat of the move just played. The checker walks this tree under
guards: past the second reply, every move of $\sigma$ wins outright or
leaves a threat, every non-winning move and every threat cell lies in
$\Board_6$, and the opponent never has a threat. A reply that blocks
nothing leaves a threat standing, which the strategy completes next
move, so every omitted reply loses in two plies.

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:forced}
Let $\sigma$ be a strategy that, whenever our stones lie in $\Board_6$
and some cell completes one of our squares, plays such a cell. If the
forced tree of $\sigma$ on $\Board_n$ passes the guards and every
branch ends in a first-player square, then
$\texttt{WinsWith}\ \sigma\ n\ [\,]$: the tree of $\sigma$'s own moves
against every reply is winning.
\end{proposition}

The hypothesis is what the two-ply argument needs: if the winning tree
is to be $\sigma$'s own, $\sigma$ must be the one to complete the
standing threat. The rule satisfies it by its first clause, once the
executable scan it uses is known to agree with the checker's, which is
proved. Section~\ref{sec:lean} defines \texttt{WinsWith} and names the
theorems.

The rule passes the forced tree on every board from $6$ to $17$;
Table~\ref{tab:runs} gives the number of reply pairs at the root and
the time of the compiled checker, together with the full-tree check of
the $5 \times 5$ table. The rule exists three times and the checkers
twice: a Python twin used while finding the rule and the table, the
Lean definitions, and a TypeScript copy of the rule that drives the
interactive page accompanying the paper. The Python and TypeScript copies are compared with the Lean rule
on the line it commits to for every reply pair of the $6 \times 6$
board and for the anchored positions of Section~\ref{sec:uniform}, and
the Python verifier, which walks the full tree rather than the forced
skeleton, confirms the win on $\Board_6$ with the same longest game.
Only the Lean runs enter the proof.

\section{Every larger board}\label{sec:uniform}

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:uniform}
For every $n \ge 17$ the first player following the rule wins on
$\Board_n$.
\end{theorem}

Checking boards one at a time can never finish, and the difficulty a
large board adds is room: stones placed far from the corner region
belong to squares that exist only out there, and if one of those
squares reached three corners, the rule would have to answer far from
its lines. Lemma~\ref{lem:dichotomy} says this can happen only through
a hidden corner, and the rule never surrenders one. The proof is a
simulation. Figure~\ref{fig:zones} shows the regions it lives in.

\begin{figure}[ht]
\centering
\input{fig-zones}
\caption{The regions of the argument, drawn on $\Board_{17}$. The rule
plays in $\Board_6$; by Lemma~\ref{lem:span} every square through two
stones of $\Board_6$ lies in $\Board_{12}$; a stone beyond $\Board_{17}$
shares no square with two blocks and matters only through the hidden
corner.}
\label{fig:zones}
\end{figure}

In a position the forced run can reach, the opponent's lasting stones
are the two free replies and forced blocks in $\Board_6$. Replies
inside $\Board_{17}$ are read verbatim; a reply beyond it is replaced,
for the base run, by a stand-in, a fixed cell beyond $\Board_{17}$ when
the pair has no hidden corner and an anchor cell with the same hidden
corner when it has one. The base run is on $\Board_{23}$, the least
board holding every anchor. Safety at every node reduces to
Lemma~\ref{lem:dichotomy}: an opponent square is either inside
$\Board_{17}$, and excluded by the base run, or goes through the hidden
corner. Pairs with a hidden corner are covered by three families of
anchored runs, one per placement of the pair on the corners of its
square, over all coordinates: $\anchoredPositions$ positions, each a
run of the checker with the hidden corner as an additional guard.

The formal simulation theorem is stated for any strategy $\sigma$ and
concludes that $\sigma$'s own play wins from the real history. Beyond
the runs it has two hypotheses about $\sigma$: that $\sigma$ takes its
completions, as in Proposition~\ref{prop:forced}, and agreement,
\[
  \sigma\,(\text{real history}) \;=\; \sigma\,(\text{base history})
\]
at every node, the base history being the real one with the two replies
replaced by their stand-ins. For the rule this follows from what it
reads. Stand-ins and anchors lie beyond $\Board_{17}$, as do the stones
they replace, so both drop out of the abstraction; a reply inside
$\Board_{17}$ is its own stand-in; and the stand-ins have the same
hidden corner as the real pair, which for the anchors is part of what
the anchored run checks and for the keyless stand-ins is a short
computation. So the abstractions agree, and the rule's move is a
function of the abstraction and of the first reply's second move, which
agrees too because every reply beyond $\Board_6$ has the default second
move. A case analysis over the reply pairs assembles Theorem~\ref{thm:uniform}
from this agreement and the simulation theorem.

\begin{remark}\label{rem:anchors}
The anchor board $23$, the stand-ins $(22,22)$ and $(22,21)$, and the
anchor cells at coordinates $17$ and above are choices of the proof,
not of the rule (Figure~\ref{fig:anchors}). An anchor for the hidden
corner $(h_1,h_2)$ of a far pair sits at $(h_1+17, h_2+17)$ and
$(h_1+17, h_2)$, so it reaches coordinate $22$ at most; the anchors of
the two other families reach $a + w - h \le 21$; and the stand-ins are
placed at $22$, beyond every anchor. So $23$ is the least board that
holds them all, and any larger board would serve as the base. The rule
itself has two board constants, the region $6$ and the base $17$, with
$12 = 2 \cdot 6$ marking the stones that can have a hidden corner, and
one search constant, the three tempo moves.
\end{remark}

Theorem~\ref{thm:rule} follows from the checked runs at $6$ to $17$
with Proposition~\ref{prop:forced} and from Theorem~\ref{thm:uniform}
for $n \ge 17$, and
Theorem~\ref{thm:main} from Propositions~\ref{prop:small}
and~\ref{prop:five} and Theorem~\ref{thm:rule} with the moves
forgotten.

\section{Formalization}\label{sec:lean}

The Lean development defines cells, boards, and squares as in
Section~\ref{sec:defs}, and the winning predicate \texttt{P1CanWin} as
an inductive family whose derivations are exactly strategy trees: one
first-player move per node, every reply covered, draws failures by
construction. The second player's win and each player's ability to
secure a draw are defined from it by swapping roles after a move, and
strategy stealing and determinacy are proved against these definitions
with no computation. These definitions, a few dozen lines, are the
whole specification; everything else is proved against them.

The predicate behind Theorem~\ref{thm:rule} is \texttt{WinsWith}. For a
strategy $\sigma$, a board size $n$, and a history $h$ with the first
player to move, $\texttt{WinsWith}\ \sigma\ n\ h$ holds if $\sigma$'s
move at $h$ is legal and completes a square, or is legal, admits a
reply, and every reply completes nothing and leads to a history at
which \texttt{WinsWith} holds again. It has the constructors of
\texttt{P1CanWin} with one extra premise on each, that the move is the
one $\sigma$ returns, and implies \texttt{P1CanWin} by forgetting that
premise. A checker that plays $\sigma$ at every node proves
\texttt{WinsWith} only if its soundness proof never invents a move; the
one place the forced checker's proof did, the two-ply win against a
reply that blocks nothing, is where Proposition~\ref{prop:forced} needs
its hypothesis.

Three executable checkers are proved sound: \texttt{check}, which
replays a table against all reply sequences (\texttt{check\_sound});
the forced checker, generic over strategies
(\texttt{checkForcedS\_winsWith}, Proposition~\ref{prop:forced}); and
\texttt{refute} (\texttt{refute\_sound}). Each proof is an induction
over the checker's recursion, whose guards supply the premises of the
constructors, so soundness never inspects the strategy; the recursion
descends on the bound $n^2 + 1$ on the length of a game, and
exhausting it returns failure, which can cost completeness but never
soundness. The rule is the definition \texttt{plusMove},
$\ruleLeanLines$ lines; its threat scan agrees with the checker's
(\texttt{plusMove\_takesWins}), the agreement lemma is
\texttt{plusMove\_agree}, the simulation theorem is \texttt{simulate},
and Theorem~\ref{thm:uniform} is
\texttt{plus\_wins\_with\_of\_seventeen\_le}, in about $\plusLeanLines$
further lines. Theorem~\ref{thm:rule} is \texttt{plus\_wins\_with} and
Theorem~\ref{thm:main} is \texttt{outcome}, whose first half is
\texttt{p1\_wins\_iff}. No table appears in the statement or the proof
of Theorem~\ref{thm:rule}.

Concrete boards are discharged by native evaluation
(\texttt{native\_decide}): the refutations at $1$ to $4$, the
$5 \times 5$ run, one evaluation for the rule's runs at sizes $6$ to
$16$, one for $17$, one for the anchor board, and one per anchored
family. The axioms of \texttt{outcome} and \texttt{plus\_wins\_with} are
therefore \texttt{propext}, \texttt{Classical.choice},
\texttt{Quot.sound}, and one instance of Lean's evaluation axiom per
certificate: the kernel checks the soundness theorems but not the
evaluations, which the Lean compiler and runtime perform, the standard
trust base of large computer-assisted proofs in Lean. The development
builds in about $\buildMinutes$ minutes on a laptop, almost all of it
the evaluations, and accompanies the paper as supplementary material
with the Python code: the $5 \times 5$ solver and its table, the twin of
the rule, and the scripts that produce Table~\ref{tab:lines}, the
numbers in this paper, and the board diagrams. The checker executable
reproduces the runs of Table~\ref{tab:runs}, and the interactive page
plays the rule and shows at each move the line it is following.

\section{How the rule was found}\label{sec:how}

An earlier version of this work proved Theorem~\ref{thm:main} with a
lookup table for the boards from $6 \times 6$ up: several thousand
forcing plans, one per pair of opponent replies, found by search on the
$15 \times 15$ board and confined to the $6 \times 6$ corner. Reading
the plans rather than counting them, almost all began with an arm of
the side-$1$ plus at $(2,2)$ or $(3,2)$, the shortest ones were that
plus, and the long ones, replayed in the positions where they were
used, either forced a block so as to place a stone that a later square
needed or played the arms of the side-$2$ plus at $(3,2)$ one at a
time. A first attempt at a rule scored moves by the number of threats
they made available and failed on a few dozen reply pairs, all with
both replies near the opening, where the right move is a preparation
whose value a one-move lookahead does not see. Asking instead which
plus can be completed by force, after a few tempo moves, gave a rule
that wins them all; the order of preference, fewer tempo moves and then
a smaller plus, keeps the lines short and makes the choice unambiguous,
which the formal development needs. Three tempo moves are necessary and
sufficient, and the base board of the abstraction was set at $17$ after
$12$ failed the forced-tree check.

\section{Open questions}\label{sec:open}

The rule wins by ply $\latestPly$. What is the least ply by which the
first player can force a win on $\Board_n$, and is there a rule of
comparable length that attains it on every board? Other orderings of
the lines, or other target shapes, might shorten the longest games at
the cost of a longer statement.

The second move has seven exceptions. Is there an opening, or an
opening with a fixed second move, from which a rule of this kind wins
with none? The search that fixed the second move tried the four
neighbours of the opening in one order and did not vary the opening.

The rule does not apply to $\Board_5$, where no purely forcing strategy
from $(2,2)$ exists and the table needs quiet moves at branch points. A short rule for $\Board_5$ is open.

Finally, the constants. The region $6$ cannot shrink for a purely
forcing strategy from $(2,2)$ with the second moves searched here, and
the base $17$ is least for Lemma~\ref{lem:dichotomy}; whether a rule
can read less than the opponent's stones inside $\Board_{17}$, for
instance only those inside $\Board_6$ with a summary of the rest, is
open.
