\begin{abstract}
Two players alternately claim cells of an $n \times n$ grid; the first
to hold all four corners of an axis-parallel square wins, and a full
board without one is a draw. The first player has a winning strategy
if and only if $n \ge 5$, which answers a question of Erickson. For
$n \ge 6$ the strategy is a rule with no lookup table, built around the
\emph{plus}, a held stone with the four cells at a common distance from
it along the axes: open at $(2,2)$, answer the first reply with a
fixed second move, take a completion or a block whenever one exists,
and otherwise play at most three single forcing moves and then the
arms of a plus. The rule reads only its own stones, the second player's
stones inside a $17 \times 17$ board, and one derived cell, the corner
at which a forced block would give the second player a threat the rule
does not read; it never forces a block there, and a simulation theorem
carries a checked run on the $23 \times 23$ board to every board of
size at least $17$. The proofs are formalized
in Lean~4, and the theorem for the rule states that the tree of its
own moves is winning.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Two players alternately claim empty cells of an $n \times n$ grid, the
first player moving first. A player wins upon holding all four corners
of a square with horizontal and vertical sides, of any size: the cells
$(r,c)$, $(r,c+s)$, $(r+s,c)$, $(r+s,c+s)$ for some $s \ge 1$. If the
board fills with no player holding such a square, the game is a draw.

Erickson posed the game and asked for the outcome under optimal play
\cite{erickson-pearls, opg}. It is an achievement game in the sense of
Harary \cite{harary}, played in the Maker--Maker convention: both
players pursue the same goal, and a player who loses the race may still
secure a draw. The convention goes back to the positional games of
Hales and Jewett \cite{hales-jewett}; a recent unified treatment of
achievement conventions, with the strategy-stealing theory in the
generality used here, is \cite{galliot-senizergues}, and the general
theory of positional games is in Beck \cite{beck}.

Two boundary results were known. Jenrich \cite{jenrich} reported
computer searches showing that the second player can secure a draw for
$n = 3, 4$ and that the first player can force a win for $n = 5$.
Bacher and Eliahou \cite{bacher-eliahou} proved that every two-coloring
of the $15 \times 15$ grid contains four equal-colored cells at the
corners of an axis-parallel square, and that $15$ is least with this
property. Since no completed game on a board of size $15$ or more is
square-free, strategy stealing gives the first player a win there.
Between these results nothing was published: for $6 \le n \le 14$
neither a winning strategy nor a drawing strategy was known
\cite{opg}, and no coloring argument can reach this range, since Bacher
and Eliahou also exhibit square-free two-colorings of the $14 \times 14$
board. Concurrently with the present work, Adler \cite{adler} announced
computer searches settling these nine sizes as first-player wins, with
full strategy trees certified for $n = 6$ and $7$ and search verdicts
for the rest, one search per size; Adler notes that the results are
unrefereed and names a conceptual proof with an explicit strategy as
the open direction.

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:main}
In the square achievement game on the $n \times n$ board, the first
player has a winning strategy if and only if $n \ge 5$. For $n \le 4$
the game is drawn under optimal play: each player can secure at least a
draw.
\end{theorem}

Relative to Adler's announcement, what is new is a single strategy
that wins every board from $6 \times 6$ up, stated in a paragraph, and
a proof checked by a proof assistant. The strategy is a rule built
around one shape, the plus of Definition~\ref{def:plus}: the arms of a
plus force the second player's replies one at a time, and the last arm
threatens two squares at once.

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:rule}
For every $n \ge 6$, the first player following the plus rule of
Section~\ref{sec:rule} wins the square achievement game on the
$n \times n$ board.
\end{theorem}

Theorem~\ref{thm:rule} is proved by checking the rule's forced tree on
every board from $6$ to $17$ (Section~\ref{sec:verify}) and by a
simulation theorem, stated for any strategy, that carries a checked run
on the $23 \times 23$ board to every board of size at least $17$
(Section~\ref{sec:uniform}). Theorem~\ref{thm:main} follows with an exhaustive
refutation for $n \le 4$ and a strategy table for $n = 5$, whose play
from the opening $(2,2)$ is not purely forcing
(Section~\ref{sec:small}). Everything is formalized in Lean~4
\cite{lean4} on mathlib \cite{mathlib} (Section~\ref{sec:lean}). The
formal theorem for the rule states that the tree of the rule's own
moves is winning, not only that a winning tree exists; the runs of the
checkers are discharged by evaluation, and the two properties of the
rule that the simulation theorem needs are proved as lemmas about its
definition. The rule was extracted from an earlier lookup-table
strategy, almost all of whose forcing sequences were arms of a side-$1$
plus at $(2,2)$ or $(3,2)$, the rest preparing the side-$2$ plus at
$(3,2)$.

The first version of the search in \cite{jenrich} contained an error
that was corrected later. The formalization makes every search here
discharge a statement about the game rather than about a program.

\section{Definitions}\label{sec:defs}

Cells are pairs $(r,c)$ of non-negative integers, row then column,
with row $0$ at the top and column $0$ at the left in the figures, and
$\Board_n = \{0,\dots,n-1\}^2$. For $s \ge 1$ the square anchored at
$(r,c)$ with side $s$ is
$\Sq(r,c,s) = \{(r,c), (r,c+s), (r+s,c), (r+s,c+s)\}$; a set of cells
contains a square if it contains some $\Sq(r,c,s)$. The case $s = 1$ is
allowed, so the smallest winning shape is a $2 \times 2$ block, the
convention of \cite{bacher-eliahou, jenrich}; tilted squares do not
count. A \emph{position} is a pair of disjoint subsets of $\Board_n$,
the stones of the two players. The game is finite with perfect
information, so exactly one of three holds: the first player can force
a win, the second can, or both can force at least a draw.

The first player \emph{threatens} at a cell $z$ if $z$ is empty and $z$
together with three of the first player's stones is a square; the
second player \emph{blocks} by playing $z$. A move that makes exactly
one threat is \emph{forcing}; a move that makes two threats wins, since
one block leaves the other. A \emph{ply} is one move of either player,
so the first player moves at the odd plies. The rule of
Section~\ref{sec:rule} plays inside the \emph{region} $\Board_6$ and
reads the second player's stones inside the \emph{base board}
$\Board_{17}$.

\subsection{Pluses}

\begin{definition}\label{def:plus}
The \emph{plus} of side $s$ at the \emph{centre} $c = (r,k)$ has four
\emph{arms}, the cells $(r-s,k)$, $(r+s,k)$, $(r,k-s)$, $(r,k+s)$, and
four \emph{diagonals}, the cells $(r \pm s, k \pm s)$
(Figure~\ref{fig:anatomy}). Two arms are \emph{perpendicular} if one
is above or below the centre and the other to its left or right; the
diagonal between them is the cell in the row of one and the column of
the other. A plus lies in the region if its arms and diagonals do, that
is, $s \le r, k$ and $r+s, k+s \le 5$.
\end{definition}

The region holds $16$ pluses of side $1$, with centres in
$\{1,\dots,4\}^2$, and four of side $2$, with centres in $\{2,3\}^2$;
side $3$ does not fit.

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:arms}
Suppose the first player holds the centre $c$ and one arm $a$ of a plus
of side $s$, that its four diagonals are empty, and that the second
player holds none of its arms. Playing an empty arm $b$ perpendicular
to $a$ threatens at the
diagonal between $a$ and $b$. If the first player also holds the arm
$a'$ opposite to $a$, playing $b$ threatens at two cells, the diagonals
between $a$ and $b$ and between $a'$ and $b$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Say $a = (r-s,k)$ and $b = (r,k-s)$. Then $c$, $a$, $b$ and
$d = (r-s,k-s)$ are the corners of $\Sq(r-s,k-s,s)$, so with $c, a, b$
held and $d$ empty the first player threatens at $d$. With
$a' = (r+s,k)$ also held, $c$, $a'$, $b$ and $(r+s,k-s)$ are the
corners of $\Sq(r,k-s,s)$, and $(r+s,k-s) \ne d$. The other placements
are symmetric.
\end{proof}

So from a centre and one arm the first player wins in three moves
against a second player who has no threat and whose blocks make none.
The two arms perpendicular to the held arm each force a block on a
diagonal, and the opposite arm threatens at two diagonals at once
(Figure~\ref{fig:basic}). The opening $(2,2)$ and the second move
$(3,2)$ are the centre and an arm of the side-$1$ plus at $(2,2)$, and
an arm and the centre of the side-$1$ plus at $(3,2)$.

\subsection{Far stones and the hidden corner}

The rule's stones never leave $\Board_6$, and the second player's
stones in a position the rule reaches are two free replies and forced
blocks, the blocks in $\Board_6$ as well.

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:span}
Let $x \ne y$ be distinct corners of a square $\Sq(r,c,s)$ with
$x, y \in \Board_B$. Then $\Sq(r,c,s) \subseteq \Board_{2B}$, and every
corner of $\Sq(r,c,s)$ has its row or its column indexed below $B$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Two distinct corners either share a coordinate and differ by $s$ in the
other, or differ by $s$ in both; either way $s < B$. Each anchor
coordinate is bounded by the corresponding coordinate of a corner, so
$r, c < B$, and the containment follows. If $x$ and $y$ share a row,
the two remaining corners inherit their columns, both below $B$; a
shared column is symmetric; and if $x$ and $y$ are diagonally opposite,
all four corners lie in $\Board_B$.
\end{proof}

With $B = 6$, two stones of the region lie in a common square only if
that square lies in $\Board_{12}$. Hence a second-player stone beyond
$\Board_{17}$ shares no square with two blocks, and a second-player
threat through such a stone and a block has the other free reply as
its third corner and an empty fourth (Figure~\ref{fig:latent}). The
block in that situation is the one cell through which stones beyond
$\Board_{17}$ can affect the game.

\begin{definition}\label{def:latent}
Let $x$ and $y$ be two cells each having a coordinate at least $12$.
Their \emph{hidden corner} is a cell $b \in \Board_6$ such that
$b$, $x$, $y$ are three corners of one square. The \emph{abstraction}
of a position reached after the second player's second move consists
of the first player's stones, the second player's stones inside
$\Board_{17}$, and the hidden corner of the second player's first two
stones, if they have one.
\end{definition}

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:dichotomy}
Two cells $x \ne y$ have at most one hidden corner. If $\Sq(r,c,s)$ has
a corner $b \in \Board_6$, has $x$ and $y$ among its other corners, and
is not contained in $\Board_{17}$, then $b = (r,c)$, $s \ge 12$, each
of $x$, $y$ has a coordinate at least $12$, and $b$ is the hidden
corner of $\{x, y\}$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Uniqueness. If $x$ and $y$ share a column, the square has side
$s = |x_1 - y_1|$, and $b$ lies in the row of a member with row below
$6$, at distance $s$ to its left or right. Only one member can have row
below $6$: if both did, then $s < 6$ and the shared column, being the
coordinate at least $12$ of either, would put both candidates for $b$
at column $7$ or more. For that member the cell to the right is
excluded too, since it lies in $\Board_6$ only if the member's column
is below $6$. A shared row is symmetric. If $x$ and $y$ are diagonally
opposite, the two remaining corners are $(x_1, y_2)$ and $(y_1, x_2)$,
of which at most one lies in $\Board_6$, since otherwise all four
coordinates of $x$ and $y$ would be below $6$.

For the second claim, a second corner in $\Board_6$ would put the
square inside $\Board_{12}$ by Lemma~\ref{lem:span}, against the
hypothesis; so $b$ is alone in the region. Were $b$ not $(r,c)$, the
corner $(r,c)$ would have both coordinates at most those of $b$ and
would lie in $\Board_6$; so $b = (r,c)$. The corner opposite $b$ has
both coordinates at most $5 + s$, and the square leaves $\Board_{17}$
only if a coordinate reaches $17$, so $s \ge 12$. Each of $x$, $y$ has
a coordinate equal to $r + s$ or $c + s$, hence at least $12$, and $b$
is their hidden corner by definition.
\end{proof}

The rule therefore never makes a threat whose only block is the hidden
corner (Definitions~\ref{def:tempo} and~\ref{def:line}). It applies the
restriction whenever the pair has a hidden corner, also when the square
through it stays inside $\Board_{17}$ and the restriction is not
needed.

\begin{remark}\label{rem:base}
The base $17 = 6 + 2 \cdot 6 - 1$ is least for
Lemma~\ref{lem:dichotomy}. With base $15$ or $16$ in place of $17$, the
replies $(0,16)$ and $(11,5)$ span the square $\Sq(0,5,11)$ through the
region cell $(0,5)$, which fits in neither $\Board_{15}$ nor
$\Board_{16}$, while $(11,5)$ has no coordinate at least $12$.
\end{remark}

\subsection{Tempo moves and lines}

When no plus can be completed directly, because the second player holds
an arm or a diagonal or because the arms would not force, the rule
first plays moves that each force a block and so add a stone.

\begin{definition}\label{def:tempo}
A \emph{tempo move} in a position is a cell of $\Board_6$ such that,
after it is played, the second player has no threat and the first
player has exactly one, at a cell other than the hidden corner.
\end{definition}

In a position where the second player has a threat, a tempo move must
remove it, and the only way is to occupy its cell; so the second player
has exactly one threat there and the tempo move is its block.

\begin{definition}\label{def:line}
A \emph{line} from a position consists of some tempo moves, each
followed by its block, and then the arms the first player does not yet
hold of one plus in the region whose centre the first player holds and
none of whose arms the second player holds, in some order, played until
an arm makes at least two threats: after each move of the line the
second player has no threat, and each arm before that one makes exactly
one threat, at a cell other than the hidden corner. The \emph{key} of a
line is the triple
\[
  (\text{number of tempo moves},\ \text{number of arms not held},\
  \text{side of the plus}).
\]
The rule prefers the smallest key in lexicographic order, then the
smaller centre in cell order (row, then column), then the line its
search generates first.
\end{definition}

The arms after the one that makes two threats are part of the line and
of its key but are never played. A block may give the second player a
threat; the next move of the line is then a tempo move that takes it.
The rule searches for lines with at most three tempo moves. Whether a
line exists, and which is preferred, depends only on the abstraction of
the position.

\section{The plus rule}\label{sec:rule}

Algorithm~\ref{alg:move} is the move function and
Algorithm~\ref{alg:line} the line search it calls once, after the
second reply. Both are stated as in the formal development. The second
move $x_2(o_1)$ against a first reply $o_1$ is $(3,2)$ unless $o_1$ is
one of seven cells, for which Figure~\ref{fig:second} gives the move.

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{The move function.}\label{alg:move}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\If{some empty cell completes a square of the first player}
  \State play the least such cell
\ElsIf{some empty cell completes a square of the second player}
  \State play the least such cell
\ElsIf{the first player has no stone}
  \State play $(2,2)$
\ElsIf{the first player does not hold $x_2(o_1)$}
  \State play $x_2(o_1)$
\Else
  \State $L \gets$ the line of Algorithm~\ref{alg:line} from the
    abstraction after the second reply
  \State play the first cell of $L$ the first player does not hold
\EndIf
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

Lines 1 to 4 scan the squares of the abstraction: the first player's,
which lie in $\Board_6$, and the second player's with all corners
inside $\Board_{17}$. A second-player square with a corner beyond
$\Board_{17}$ would go through the hidden corner, where the rule never
lets a block land, and the checker of Section~\ref{sec:verify}
confirms at every node that the second player has no threat at all. If
Algorithm~\ref{alg:line} returns no line the rule is undefined; the
checker certifies that this never happens at a position the rule
reaches.

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{The line search, from an abstract position $A$.}\label{alg:line}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\State $F \gets [(A, \varepsilon)]$
  \Comment{positions reached, with the tempo moves that reached them}
\For{$d = 0, 1, 2, 3$}
  \State $R \gets \varnothing$
  \ForAll{$(B, p) \in F$ in order, such that the second player has no threat in $B$}
    \ForAll{pluses $P$ in the region whose centre the first player holds in $B$}
      \State $T \gets$ the arms of $P$ the first player does not hold in $B$
      \If{$T \ne \varnothing$ and the second player holds no cell of $T$}
        \State $\pi \gets$ the first ordering of $T$ such that $p\,\pi$ is a line from $A$
        \If{$\pi$ exists} record $p\,\pi$ in $R$ with the key $(|T|, \text{side of } P, \text{centre of } P)$ \EndIf
      \EndIf
    \EndFor
  \EndFor
  \If{$R \ne \varnothing$} \Return the first line recorded in $R$ among those with the smallest key \EndIf
  \State $F \gets$ the successors of the members of $F$, in order
\EndFor
\State \Return no line
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

The successors of $(B, p)$ in line 11 are the pairs $(B', p\,m)$ for
the tempo moves $m$ in $B$, taken in cell order, where $B'$ is $B$
after $m$ and its block. They are taken from every member of $F$,
including positions in which the second player has a threat; such a
position offers no plus (line 4), but its tempo moves, which must
remove the threat, are explored. Line 8 tries the arm orders in a fixed
enumeration, so that two implementations choose the same line.

The Python implementation of the rule was run over every reply pair on
each board from $6$ to $17$ and on the anchor board $\Board_{23}$, and
from every anchored position of Section~\ref{sec:uniform}, recording
the line, its tempo moves, and the ply of the win when the second
player blocks. Every board from $6$ to $17$, the anchor board, whose
$\pairsAnchor$ pairs have $\anchorAbstractions$ distinct abstractions,
and every anchored position play the same $\distinctLines$ lines. Every
abstraction that occurs on a board beyond $17$ occurs on the anchor
board or in an anchored position (Section~\ref{sec:uniform}), so the
same holds on every board. Table~\ref{tab:lines} splits the pairs by
the number of tempo moves. Every pair won by a side-$1$ plus needs no
tempo move and every pair won by a side-$2$ plus needs at least one, so
the first column also counts the side-$1$ pairs. In
$\blocksThatThreaten$ of the $\pairsSix$ pairs on $\Board_6$, all with
three tempo moves, a block gives the second player a threat that the
next tempo move takes. The longest line, the second game of
Table~\ref{tab:games}, has $\longestLineMoves$ first-player moves after
the opening pair, and every line ends by ply $\latestPly$ when the
second player blocks; a reply that blocks nothing leaves a threat that
the rule completes at its next move. So the rule wins every game by the
$\latestPly$th ply. This bound is computed from the $\distinctLines$
lines and is not part of the formal theorem.

\section{Small boards and the \texorpdfstring{$5 \times 5$}{5 x 5} board}\label{sec:small}

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:stealing}
The second player has no winning strategy.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Suppose the second player had a winning strategy $\tau$. Let the first
player open with an arbitrary move and thereafter follow $\tau$ as if
the extra stone were absent, making a further arbitrary move whenever
$\tau$ selects an occupied cell. The winning condition is monotone in a
player's stone set, so surplus stones never hurt (a surplus stone may
itself complete a square, which ends the game in the first player's
favour), and the first player wins; but $\tau$ was assumed to win
against all first-player play, a contradiction.
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:small}
For $n \le 4$ the first player has no winning strategy. With
Lemma~\ref{lem:stealing}, these games are drawn under optimal play:
each player can secure at least a draw.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
For $n \le 2$ the board has at most four cells, so neither player ever
holds four. For $n = 3, 4$ the claim is a statement about a finite game
tree, verified by an exhaustive search that is part of the formal
development: for every first-player move the search exhibits a reply
after which the first player still cannot force a win. The result
agrees with \cite{jenrich}.
The step from ``neither player can force a win'' to ``each player can
secure a draw'' is determinacy of the finite game, proved by induction
on the number of empty cells.
\end{proof}

\Needspace*{6\baselineskip}
\begin{proposition}\label{prop:five}
The first player has a winning strategy on $\Board_5$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
The strategy is a table, extracted from a full solution of the
$5 \times 5$ game ($\fiveTablePositions$ positions solved, with draws
scored against the first player) and verified by a checker that walks
the complete game tree, every reply at every node. Like the rule, it
completes a square when it can and otherwise blocks one before
consulting the table. It opens at $(2,2)$, gives a second move for each
of the $\fiveTableBranches$ first replies, and after the second reply
plays a sequence of forcing moves ending in a double threat where one
exists. For $\fiveNoForcing$ of the
reply pairs, with the table's second move, no continuation in which
every first-player move makes a threat wins, so the table plays a
\emph{quiet} move there, one making no threat, and continues with a
sub-table indexed by the reply (Figure~\ref{fig:five}). That no
forcing continuation exists for these $\fiveNoForcing$ pairs is a
computation of the solver and is not formalized; the win of the table
is.
\end{proof}

\section{Verification}\label{sec:verify}

Fix a strategy $\sigma$ and a board size $n$. The \emph{full tree} of
$\sigma$ on $\Board_n$ is the game tree in which the first player's
moves are given by $\sigma$ and the second player's moves range over
all empty cells; $\sigma$ \emph{verifies} on $\Board_n$ if every branch
ends in a first-player square, and then the first player wins. The
$5 \times 5$ table is checked on its full tree.

\Needspace*{10\baselineskip}
The rule is checked on a smaller tree. The \emph{forced tree} of
$\sigma$ on $\Board_n$ has the first player playing $\sigma$'s move,
the second player's replies ranging over all empty cells at the first
two levels, and below every later node only over the cells that block
a threat of the move just played. The checker walks this tree under
guards:
\begin{enumerate}
\item[(G1)] after the second reply, every move of $\sigma$ wins
  outright or makes a threat;
\item[(G2)] every non-winning move and every threat cell lies in
  $\Board_6$;
\item[(G3)] after every move of $\sigma$ the second player has no
  threat;
\item[(G4)] optionally, for a given cell $g$, no move of $\sigma$ has
  $g$ as its only threat cell.
\end{enumerate}
A reply that blocks nothing leaves a threat standing, which the
strategy completes at its next move, so every omitted reply loses in
two plies.

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:forced}
Let $\sigma$ be a strategy that, whenever the first player's stones lie
in $\Board_6$ and some cell completes one of the first player's
squares, plays such a cell. If the forced tree of $\sigma$ on
$\Board_n$ passes the guards and every branch ends in a first-player
square, then the tree in which the first player plays $\sigma$'s move
at every node is winning; in particular the first player wins on
$\Board_n$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
This is the soundness theorem of the forced checker, an induction over
the checker's recursion (Section~\ref{sec:lean}). The hypothesis makes
$\sigma$ the player who completes the standing threat against a reply
that blocks nothing, so that the winning tree is $\sigma$'s own. The
rule satisfies it by its first clause, whose threat scan agrees with
the checker's whenever the first player's stones lie in $\Board_6$;
this is proved as a lemma.
\end{proof}

The rule passes the forced tree on every board from $6$ to $17$, one
run of the checker per board with $(n^2-1)(n^2-3)$ reply pairs at the
root (Table~\ref{tab:runs} in Section~\ref{sec:lean}). A Python
implementation of the rule and a full-tree verifier were used while the
rule was being found. The checker executable prints the line the Lean
rule commits to for every reply pair of $\Board_6$ and for every
anchored position of Section~\ref{sec:uniform}, and a test of the
Python implementation reproduces every one; the full-tree verifier
confirms the win on $\Board_6$. Only the Lean runs enter the proof.

\section{Boards of size at least 17}\label{sec:uniform}

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:uniform}
For every $n \ge 17$ the first player following the rule wins on
$\Board_n$.
\end{theorem}

By Lemma~\ref{lem:dichotomy}, a stone beyond $\Board_{17}$ can affect
the rule's play only through a hidden corner, and the rule never
forces a block there. The proof replays a checked run on $\Board_{23}$
as a run on $\Board_n$. Replies inside $\Board_{17}$ are read
verbatim. A reply beyond $\Board_{17}$ is
replaced, for the base run, by a \emph{stand-in}: a fixed cell beyond
$\Board_{17}$ when the pair has no hidden corner, and an
\emph{anchor}, a cell beyond $\Board_{17}$ that forms with the other
reply the same hidden corner, when it has one. The \emph{anchor board}
$\Board_{23}$ is the least board holding every anchor
(Remark~\ref{rem:anchors}).

A \emph{history} is the sequence of moves played so far, and for a
history $h$ and a sequence $t$ of further moves $h\,t$ is their
concatenation. A strategy $\sigma$ is given the board, and
$\sigma_n(h)$ is its move after $h$ on $\Board_n$. The guards are those
of Section~\ref{sec:verify}.

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:simulate}
Let $\sigma$ be a strategy, let $B, n \ge 17$, and let
$x_1, x_2 \in \Board_6$. Let $h = (x_1, o_1, x_2, o_2)$ be a history
on $\Board_n$ and $h' = (x_1, o_1', x_2, o_2')$ a history on
$\Board_B$ such that for each $i$ either $o_i' = o_i \in \Board_{17}$,
or $o_i \in \Board_n \setminus \Board_{17}$ and
$o_i' \in \Board_B \setminus \Board_{12}$. Let $g$ be either no cell or
a cell of $\Board_6$. Suppose that
\begin{enumerate}
\item[(i)] on every history extending $h$ in which the first player's
  stones lie in $\Board_6$, $\sigma$ plays a cell completing one of the
  first player's squares whenever one exists;
\item[(ii)] $\sigma_n(h\,t) = \sigma_B(h'\,t)$ for every sequence $t$
  of further moves;
\item[(iii)] the forced tree of $\sigma$ on $\Board_B$ below $h'$
  passes the guards, with $g$ as the cell of (G4) if $g$ is a cell, and
  every branch ends in a first-player square;
\item[(iv)] if $o_1$ or $o_2$ lies beyond $\Board_{17}$, then either
  $g$ is no cell and $\{o_1, o_2\}$ has no hidden corner
  (Definition~\ref{def:latent}), or $g$ is the hidden corner of
  $\{o_1, o_2\}$.
\end{enumerate}
Then the tree in which the first player plays $\sigma$'s move at every
node below $h$ on $\Board_n$ is winning.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
By induction along the forced tree of (iii). Consider a node of that
tree together with the corresponding real position on $\Board_n$; the
two differ only at the two reply slots, and the blocks played so far,
all in $\Board_6$ by (G2), are shared. The two positions agree inside
$\Board_{12}$: a real reply beyond $\Board_{17}$ and its stand-in both
lie outside $\Board_{12}$, and every other stone is shared. By (ii)
$\sigma$ plays the same cell $m$ in both. If $m$ completes a square in
the base position, the square has three corners among the first
player's stones, which lie in $\Board_6$, so it lies in $\Board_{12}$
by Lemma~\ref{lem:span}, and $m$ is empty and completes it on
$\Board_n$ too. Otherwise $m \in \Board_6$ by (G2), and the threat
cells of $m$ are the same on both boards, since each threat square has
three corners in $\Board_6$ and so lies in $\Board_{12}$; by (G3) the
second player has no threat in the base position.

No reply $r$ completes a second-player square on $\Board_n$. Suppose
$\Sq$ were such a square. If $\Sq \subseteq \Board_{17}$, its corners
other than $r$ are second-player stones inside $\Board_{17}$, so blocks
or replies read verbatim, all shared with the base position, since the
real replies beyond $\Board_{17}$ lie outside $\Sq$ and a stand-in only
adds a stone. Then either $r$ is a stand-in cell and the base position
holds $\Sq$, or $r$ is empty in the base position and completes $\Sq$
there. The second contradicts (G3) at the current node. So does the
first: the base position holds no second-player square, because the
last of its corners to be placed was a block, the two replies alone
being too few, and the threat it completed was standing after the move
of $\sigma$ before it. If $\Sq \not\subseteq \Board_{17}$, two blocks among its
corners would confine it to $\Board_{12}$ by Lemma~\ref{lem:span}, so
its corners are $r$, one block $d \in \Board_6$, and both replies, and
a reply lies beyond $\Board_{17}$, since three corners in $\Board_{17}$
would confine $\Sq$ there. By Lemma~\ref{lem:dichotomy} $d$ is the
hidden corner of $\{o_1, o_2\}$, which contradicts (iv): either the
pair has none, or its hidden corner is $g$, and $g$ is never a block,
because a block at $g$ arises only when $g$ is the only threat cell,
which (G4) excludes, or when the second player chooses $g$ among two
threat cells, in which case $\sigma$ completes the other by (i) and
the branch ends.

A reply that blocks nothing leaves a threat, which $\sigma$ completes
by (i). A block $r \ne g$ is a block in the base run as well, and the
induction continues along the base branch, whose invariants are
preserved: the new block lies in $\Board_6$ and is not $g$.
\end{proof}

The formal theorem is stated for any node of the base run reached by
forced play, with the blocks so far in $\Board_6$ and $g$ not among
the second player's stones, and is proved by induction on the checker's
fuel.

For the rule, hypotheses (i) and (ii) are lemmas about its definition.
For (i), the first clause of Algorithm~\ref{alg:move} plays a
completion found by the rule's threat scan, and that scan agrees with
the checker's whenever the first player's stones lie in $\Board_6$. For
(ii), the rule's move depends on a history only through its
abstraction and through the second move of the first reply. A reply
beyond $\Board_{17}$ and its stand-in both drop out of the abstraction,
a reply inside $\Board_{17}$ is its own stand-in, and the stand-in pair
is chosen with the same hidden corner as the real pair, so the two
abstractions agree at every node; the second moves agree because every
first reply beyond $\Board_6$, and every stand-in, has the default
second move $(3,2)$. Figure~\ref{fig:agreement} shows a real history
and its base history.

Pairs with a hidden corner $b = (b_1, b_2)$ are covered by three
families of \emph{anchored runs}, each a forced run of the rule on
$\Board_{23}$ from the history $((2,2), o_1', x_2(o_1'), o_2')$ with
$b$ as the cell of (G4), preceded by a check that $b$ is the hidden
corner of the anchor pair.
\begin{itemize}
\item The \emph{far} family, for both replies beyond $\Board_{17}$:
  $o_1' = (b_1 + 17, b_2 + 17)$ and $o_2' = (b_1 + 17, b_2)$, two
  corners of $\Sq(b_1, b_2, 17)$; one run per $b \in \Board_6$, $36$
  runs.
\item The \emph{exact} family, for $o_1 \in \Board_{17}$ and $o_2$
  beyond it. By Lemma~\ref{lem:dichotomy}, the square through $b$ and
  the pair is $\Sq(b_1, b_2, s)$ with $s \ge 12$, and it leaves
  $\Board_{17}$; $o_1$ is not the corner opposite $b$, whose
  coordinates bound the square, so $o_1$ lies in the row or the column
  of $b$ at distance $s$, and $o_1 \in \Board_{17}$
  puts that coordinate of $o_1$ below $17$; say $o_1 = (a, w)$ and
  $b = (a, h)$ with $a, h < 6$, $12 \le w < 17$, and $s = w - h$. Then
  $o_1' = o_1$ and $o_2' = (a + s, w)$, the corner of $\Sq(a, h, s)$
  opposite $b$, which lies beyond $\Board_{17}$ exactly when the square
  does; one run per $(a, w, h)$ with $a + w - h \ge 17$ and one per
  transposed triple, $70$ runs.
\item The \emph{far-near} family, for $o_1$ beyond $\Board_{17}$ and
  $o_2 \in \Board_{17}$: the exact family with the two replies
  exchanged, $70$ runs.
\end{itemize}
This gives $\anchoredPositions$ anchored runs in all.

\begin{proof}[Proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:uniform}]
Let $n \ge 17$. The rule opens at $(2,2)$ and answers the first reply
$o_1$ with $x_2 = x_2(o_1) \in \Board_6$; both moves are legal and
complete nothing, so it remains to show that the rule's tree below
$h = ((2,2), o_1, x_2, o_2)$ is winning for every reply pair. Apply
Theorem~\ref{thm:simulate} with $B = 23$, $\sigma$ the rule, and $h'$,
$g$ as follows, calling a reply \emph{far} if it lies beyond
$\Board_{17}$.
\begin{enumerate}
\item[(a)] Neither reply far: $h' = h$, $g$ no cell, and (iii) is the
  branch at $h$ of the forced run of the rule on $\Board_{23}$; (iv) is
  vacuous.
\item[(b)] A far reply and no hidden corner: a far $o_1$ is replaced by
  $(22,22)$ and a far $o_2$ by $(22,21)$, $g$ is no cell, and (iii) is
  again a branch of the run on $\Board_{23}$. A stand-in forms no hidden
  corner with a cell of $\Board_{17}$, and the two stand-ins form none
  with each other, so the stand-in pair has no hidden corner either.
\item[(c)] Both replies far with hidden corner $b$: the far family,
  $g = b$.
\item[(d)] $o_1 \in \Board_{17}$, $o_2$ far, hidden corner $b$: the
  exact family, $g = b$.
\item[(e)] $o_1$ far, $o_2 \in \Board_{17}$, hidden corner $b$: the
  far-near family, $g = b$.
\end{enumerate}
In (c) to (e), (iii) is the anchored run, and (iv) holds by the case
hypothesis. Hypothesis (i) is the lemma above; (ii) holds because the
real and base pairs have the same hidden corner, checked at the head of
each anchored run and shown for the stand-ins in (b), and the same
second move, so that the abstractions of $h\,t$ and $h'\,t$ agree for
every $t$.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}\label{rem:anchors}
The anchors of the far family reach coordinate $5 + 17 = 22$, those of
the other two families $a + s \le 5 + 16 = 21$, and the stand-ins sit
at $22$, so $\Board_{23}$ is the least board holding them all; any
larger board would serve. The anchor board, the stand-ins, and the
anchors are choices made in the proof, and the rule does not depend on
them.
\end{remark}

\begin{proof}[Proof of Theorems~\ref{thm:main} and~\ref{thm:rule}]
Theorem~\ref{thm:rule} follows from the checked runs at $6$ to $17$
with Proposition~\ref{prop:forced}, and from Theorem~\ref{thm:uniform}
for $n \ge 17$. Theorem~\ref{thm:main} follows from
Propositions~\ref{prop:small} and~\ref{prop:five} and from
Theorem~\ref{thm:rule} with the moves forgotten.
\end{proof}

\section{Formalization}\label{sec:lean}

The Lean development defines cells, boards, and squares as in
Section~\ref{sec:defs}, and the winning predicate \texttt{P1CanWin} as
an inductive family whose derivations are exactly strategy trees: one
first-player move per node, every reply covered, and no derivation for
a game that ends in a draw.
The second player's win and each player's ability to secure a draw are
defined from it by swapping roles after a move, and strategy stealing
(\texttt{second\_player\_no\_win}, with the monotonicity step
\texttt{P1CanWin.insert\_left}) and determinacy
(\texttt{P1CanWin.or\_p2CanDraw}) are proved against these definitions
with no computation. These definitions, a few dozen lines, are the
whole specification; everything else is proved against them.

The predicate behind Theorem~\ref{thm:rule} is \texttt{WinsWith}. For a
strategy $\sigma$, a board size $n$, and a history $h$ with the first
player to move, $\texttt{WinsWith}\ \sigma\ n\ h$ holds if $\sigma$'s
move at $h$ is legal and completes a square, or is legal, admits a
reply, and every reply completes nothing and leads to a history at
which \texttt{WinsWith} holds again. It has the constructors of
\texttt{P1CanWin} with one extra premise on each, that the move is the
one $\sigma$ returns, and implies \texttt{P1CanWin} by forgetting that
premise. A checker that plays $\sigma$ at every node proves
\texttt{WinsWith} only if its soundness proof never invents a move,
which is why Proposition~\ref{prop:forced} needs its hypothesis,
\texttt{TakesWinsOn}.

Three executable checkers are proved sound: \texttt{check}, which
replays a table against all reply sequences (\texttt{check\_sound});
the forced checker, generic over strategies
(\texttt{checkForcedS\_winsWith}, Proposition~\ref{prop:forced}); and
\texttt{refute} (\texttt{refute\_sound}, Proposition~\ref{prop:small}).
Each proof is an induction over the checker's recursion, whose guards
supply the premises of the constructors, so soundness never inspects
the strategy. The $5 \times 5$ table is
\texttt{fiveTable} and Proposition~\ref{prop:five} is
\texttt{p1\_wins\_five}.

The rule is the definition \texttt{plusMove}; the file
\texttt{Plus/Rule.lean}, which defines it with the abstraction and the
line search, is about $\ruleLeanLines$ lines.
Hypothesis (i) of Theorem~\ref{thm:simulate} for the rule is
\texttt{plusMove\_takesWins} and hypothesis (ii) is
\texttt{plusMove\_agree}; both are proved by unfolding the definition,
and neither is an evaluation. The simulation theorem is \texttt{simulate}, and
Theorem~\ref{thm:uniform} is
\texttt{plus\_wins\_with\_of\_seventeen\_le}, which dispatches the
cases (a) to (e) to \texttt{simulate} with the run on the anchor board
(\texttt{plus\_forced\_anchor}) and the three families
\texttt{farAnchorsSafe}, \texttt{exactAnchorsSafe}, and
\texttt{farNearAnchorsSafe}. Lemma~\ref{lem:span} is not a separate
Lean theorem; the containments it gives are proved where they are
used, in \texttt{three\_corners\_confine} and
\texttt{square\_filter\_confine} for squares with corners in the
region and inside \texttt{escape\_farm}. The hidden corner of a pair
is computed by \texttt{farmKey}, which the abstraction and the anchored
runs use; the Lean identifiers call the hidden corner a \emph{farm}
(\texttt{farmKey}, \texttt{Key.farm}) and the cell of (G4) \texttt{hot}.
A cell \texttt{farmKey} returns lies in $\Board_6$ and is a corner of a
square through the pair (\texttt{farmKey\_corners}), and the second
claim of Lemma~\ref{lem:dichotomy} is \texttt{escape\_farm}: for a
square as in the lemma, \texttt{farmKey} returns $b$. Hypothesis (iv)
of Theorem~\ref{thm:simulate} is stated through \texttt{farmKey}: $g$
is no cell and \texttt{farmKey} returns none, or \texttt{farmKey}
returns $g$. The uniqueness
claim is not needed by the formal proof, which reads the one value
\texttt{farmKey} returns. Lemma~\ref{lem:arms} is not used by the formal proof,
which checks the lines directly. Theorem~\ref{thm:rule} is
\texttt{plus\_wins\_with} and Theorem~\ref{thm:main} is
\texttt{outcome}, whose first half is \texttt{p1\_wins\_iff}. In sum, Theorem~\ref{thm:main} rests on Propositions~\ref{prop:small}
and~\ref{prop:five} and Theorem~\ref{thm:rule};
Theorem~\ref{thm:rule} on the runs at $6$ to $17$ with
Proposition~\ref{prop:forced} and on Theorem~\ref{thm:uniform}; and
Theorem~\ref{thm:uniform} on Lemma~\ref{lem:dichotomy},
Theorem~\ref{thm:simulate}, the two lemmas about the rule, and the
four runs on $\Board_{23}$.

\section{Open questions}

The rule wins by ply $\latestPly$. What is the least ply by which the
first player can force a win on $\Board_n$, and is there a rule of
comparable length that attains it on every board? Other orderings of
the lines, or other target shapes, might shorten the longest games at
the cost of a longer statement.

The second move has seven exceptions. Is there an opening, or an
opening with a fixed second move, from which a rule of this kind wins
with none? The search of Section~\ref{sec:rule} did not vary the
opening.

The rule does not apply to $\Board_5$, where the table needs quiet
moves. A short rule for $\Board_5$ is open.

The base $17$ is least for Lemma~\ref{lem:dichotomy}
(Remark~\ref{rem:base}). Whether a rule of this kind can play inside a
region smaller than $\Board_6$, or read less than the second player's
stones inside $\Board_{17}$, for instance only those inside $\Board_6$
with a summary of the rest, is open.
