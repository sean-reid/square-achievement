\title{The square achievement game on $n \times n$ boards}
\author{Sean Reid}
\date{August 2026}

\begin{document}

\begin{abstract}
Two players alternately claim cells of an $n \times n$ grid; the first
to hold all four corners of an axis-aligned square wins, and a full
board with no such square is a draw. Erickson asked for which $n$ the
first player has a winning strategy. The answer was known at the two
ends: draws for $n \le 4$ and a first-player win for $n = 5$ by
computer search, and a first-player win for $n \ge 15$ by a theorem of
Bacher and Eliahou together with strategy stealing. We close the
remaining range: the first player wins if and only if $n \ge 5$. The
proof for $5 \le n \le 14$ is an explicit strategy, two simple
reflexes over a fixed lookup table whose plans are confined to a
$6 \times 6$ corner region, verified by exhaustive search. All results
are formalized in Lean~4: the game, the strategy tables, and
executable checkers whose soundness is machine-checked, so each
exhaustive search yields a formal theorem. We conjecture that the same
fixed table wins for every $n \ge 6$, which would replace the
non-constructive argument for large boards by a single explicit
strategy; the supporting geometry is proved here and the conjecture is
verified for $6 \le n \le 14$.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

Two players alternately claim empty cells of an $n \times n$ grid, the
first player moving first. A player wins on holding all four corners
of a square with horizontal and vertical sides, of any size: the cells
$(r,c)$, $(r,c+s)$, $(r+s,c)$, $(r+s,c+s)$ for some $s \ge 1$. If the
board fills with no player holding such a square, the game is a draw.

Erickson posed the game and asked for the outcome under optimal play
\cite{erickson-pearls, opg}. It belongs to the family of achievement
games introduced by Harary \cite{harary}, played in the Maker--Maker
convention: both players pursue the same goal, and a player who loses
the race may still hold out for a draw. For the general theory of
positional games see Beck \cite{beck}.

Two boundary results were known. Jenrich \cite{jenrich} reported
computer searches showing that the second player can secure a draw for
$n = 3, 4$ and that the first player can force a win for $n = 5$.
Bacher and Eliahou \cite{bacher-eliahou} proved that every
two-coloring of the $15 \times 15$ grid contains four equal-colored
cells at the corners of an axis-aligned square, and that $15$ is least
with this property. Since no completed game on a board of size $15$ or
more is square-free, strategy stealing gives the first player a win
there. Between these results the problem was open: for
$6 \le n \le 14$ neither a winning strategy nor a drawing strategy was
known \cite{opg}.

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:main}
In the square achievement game on the $n \times n$ board, the first
player has a winning strategy if and only if $n \ge 5$.
\end{theorem}

The new content of Theorem~\ref{thm:main} is the range
$5 \le n \le 14$, which we settle with an explicit strategy: two
reflexes (complete a square when possible, otherwise block an imminent
completion) over a fixed lookup table of forcing plans. The plans are
confined to the $6 \times 6$ corner region of the board, and replies
far from that region are looked up through finitely many geometric
classes, so a single table serves every board size at once. For each
$n$ in range, an exhaustive search over all reply sequences certifies
that the strategy wins.

Exhaustive searches are only as trustworthy as the programs running
them, and this game has already supplied a cautionary example: the
first version of the search in \cite{jenrich} contained an error, and
its corrected form initially returned the opposite verdict for $n = 5$
before further revision. We therefore formalize everything in Lean~4
\cite{lean4}, building on mathlib \cite{mathlib}: the game itself, a
checker that replays the strategy against every reply sequence, a dual
checker refuting first-player wins on small boards, and soundness
theorems for both. Each search then discharges a statement about the
abstract game rather than about a program. The formal development
accompanies this paper as supplementary material.

The strategy-stealing argument for $n \ge 15$ proves existence without
exhibiting a strategy. Our table is a candidate replacement: we
conjecture it wins for every $n \ge 6$
(Conjecture~\ref{conj:uniform}), prove the geometric lemma that
confines its play to a bounded region, and verify the conjecture for
$6 \le n \le 14$.

\section{Definitions and conventions}\label{sec:defs}

\begin{definition}
Cells are pairs of non-negative integers, written $(r, c)$ for row and
column. The board of size $n$ is $\Board_n = \{0, \dots, n-1\}^2$. For
$s \ge 1$, the \emph{square} anchored at $(r, c)$ with side $s$ is the
four-element set
\[
  \Sq(r,c,s) \;=\; \{(r,c),\ (r,c+s),\ (r+s,c),\ (r+s,c+s)\}.
\]
A set $A$ of cells \emph{contains a square} if
$\Sq(r,c,s) \subseteq A$ for some $r, c \ge 0$ and $s \ge 1$.
\end{definition}

The case $s = 1$ is allowed: the smallest winning configuration is a
$2 \times 2$ block of cells. This is the convention of
\cite{jenrich, bacher-eliahou}.

A \emph{position} is a pair of disjoint subsets of $\Board_n$, the
stones of the first and the second player. Play alternates, the first
player moving first, each move claiming an empty cell. A player wins
at the first moment their stones contain a square; if the board fills
with neither player holding one, the game is drawn. The game is finite
with perfect information, so exactly one of three statements holds:
the first player can force a win, the second player can force a win,
or both can force at least a draw.

\begin{lemma}[strategy stealing]\label{lem:stealing}
The second player has no winning strategy.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Suppose the second player had a winning strategy $\tau$. Let the first
player open with an arbitrary move and thereafter follow $\tau$ as if
the extra stone were absent, making a further arbitrary move whenever
$\tau$ selects an occupied cell. The winning condition is monotone in
a player's stone set, so surplus stones never hurt, and the first
player wins; but $\tau$ was assumed to win against all first-player
play, a contradiction.
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:large}
For $n \ge 15$ the first player has a winning strategy.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
A completed game two-colors the board, and by \cite{bacher-eliahou}
any two-coloring of a fixed $15 \times 15$ subgrid contains a
monochromatic square. Hence no completed game on $\Board_n$ with
$n \ge 15$ is a draw. The second player cannot force a win by
Lemma~\ref{lem:stealing}, so by finiteness the first player can.
\end{proof}

\section{Small boards}\label{sec:small}

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:small}
For $n \le 4$ the first player has no winning strategy. With
Lemma~\ref{lem:stealing}, these games are drawn under optimal play.
\end{proposition}

For $n \le 2$ no square fits the board. For $n = 3, 4$ the claim is a
statement about a finite game tree, verified by an exhaustive search
that is part of the formal development (Section~\ref{sec:lean}): the
searcher shows that the player to move cannot force a win by
exhibiting, for every first-player move, a reply after which the first
player still cannot force one. Two standard reductions keep the tree
small: a player able to complete a square wins immediately, and a
player facing two distinct completion threats has lost. The
$4 \times 4$ search visits roughly $10^5$ positions.
Proposition~\ref{prop:small} confirms the report of \cite{jenrich}.

\section{The strategy}\label{sec:strategy}

The strategy has two layers.

\subsection{Reflexes}
Before consulting any table, the strategy plays
\begin{enumerate}
  \item the least cell (in lexicographic order) completing one of its
    own squares, if such a cell exists; otherwise
  \item the least cell completing an opponent square, if such a cell
    exists.
\end{enumerate}
The reflexes absorb every forced exchange: the final move of each
winning line is played by reflex~(1), and the counter-threats that
arise as the opponent's forced blocks accumulate are answered by
reflex~(2). The table therefore records only free choices.

\subsection{The table}
When no reflex applies, the strategy consults a lookup table. The
opening move is $(2,2)$. The table maps the opponent's first reply
$o_1$ to a second move, and the pair $(o_1, o_2)$ of the first two
replies to a \emph{plan}: an ordered list of cells, of which the
strategy plays the first not already held. Plans are forcing: in the
verified lines each plan move completes three corners of some square,
so every reply is forced, until some move creates two threats at once
and reflex~(1) converts.

Two features let one table serve all boards. First, every cell a plan
can play lies in the corner region $\{0,\dots,5\}^2$, so each square
the strategy builds exists on every board with $n \ge 6$; this
confinement is a checked property of the table. Second, replies
outside the region are looked up through \emph{alignment classes}: a
far reply in row $r < 6$ falls in class $(\mathrm{row},\, r)$, one in
column $c < 6$ in class $(\mathrm{col},\, c)$, and the rest in a
default class. The classes reflect the geometry of
Lemma~\ref{lem:span}: a far stone joins a square through two stones of
the corner region only when it shares a row or column with the region.

The board of size $5$ is treated separately, and necessarily so: its
table is not purely forcing. Exhaustive analysis shows that from
twenty of the possible $(o_1, o_2)$ openings no all-forcing
continuation wins on $\Board_5$. The $5 \times 5$ table therefore
contains \emph{quiet forks}, a non-threatening move together with a
sub-table indexed by the reply to it, extracted from a full solution
of the $5 \times 5$ game ($1.03 \times 10^8$ positions searched, draws
scored against the first player).

The general table holds $237$ branch keys (the first replies of the
generation board, thirteen alignment classes, and a default) with
$8{,}135$ plan entries in all; the $5 \times 5$ table holds $24$
branches. Both are included with the supplementary material in source
and machine-readable form.

\section{Verification}\label{sec:verify}

Fix a strategy $\sigma$ and a board size $n$. The \emph{verification
tree} of $\sigma$ on $\Board_n$ is the game tree in which the first
player's moves are given by $\sigma$ and the second player's moves
range over all empty cells. Say that $\sigma$ \emph{verifies} on
$\Board_n$ if every branch ends in a first-player square: no branch
reaches a second-player square, a full board, or a position where
$\sigma$ produces no legal move.

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:verify}
If $\sigma$ verifies on $\Board_n$, then the first player wins the
square achievement game on $\Board_n$.
\end{proposition}

Proposition~\ref{prop:verify} is the soundness of exhaustive search;
its formal counterpart is the theorem \texttt{check\_sound} of
Section~\ref{sec:lean}. The strategy of Section~\ref{sec:strategy}
verifies on $\Board_n$ for every $5 \le n \le 14$, which together with
Propositions~\ref{prop:small} and~\ref{prop:large} proves
Theorem~\ref{thm:main}. Table~\ref{tab:runs} reports the sizes of the
verification trees and the running times of the compiled formal
checker.

\section{Formalization}\label{sec:lean}

The Lean development defines cells in the quarter-plane, boards as
finite subsets, squares as in Section~\ref{sec:defs}, and the winning
predicate as an inductive family: the first player \emph{can win} from
a position if some legal move completes one of their squares, or some
legal move admits at least one reply and every reply both fails to
complete an opponent square and leads to a position from which the
first player can win. A derivation of the predicate is literally a
strategy tree, one first-player move per node with all replies
covered, and draws are failures by construction.

Two executable checkers are proved sound against this semantics:
\texttt{check}, which replays a strategy table against all reply
sequences, with
\[
  \texttt{check\_sound} \;:\;
  \texttt{check}\ t\ n = \texttt{true}
  \;\longrightarrow\; \text{the first player can win on } \Board_n,
\]
and \texttt{refute}, the dual search of Section~\ref{sec:small}, with
\[
  \texttt{refute\_sound} \;:\;
  \texttt{refute}\ n = \texttt{true}
  \;\longrightarrow\; \text{the first player cannot win on } \Board_n.
\]

One design decision kept both proofs short. The checker validates
every move it plays: legality is enforced by guards, completions are
detected by an executable scan proved equivalent to the abstract
predicate, and replies are enumerated exhaustively. Soundness
therefore never inspects the strategy; any table surviving the guards
yields a winning derivation, and the tables enter the development
purely as data.

Concrete board sizes are discharged by native evaluation
(\texttt{native\_decide}): for instance, the statement that the first
player can win on $\Board_5$ is \texttt{check\_sound} applied to one
evaluated run of \texttt{check}. The axiom footprint of these theorems
is \texttt{propext}, \texttt{Classical.choice}, \texttt{Quot.sound},
and Lean's evaluation axiom for compiled reduction, the usual trust
base for kernel-external computation.

\section{Toward a uniform strategy}\label{sec:uniform}

Proposition~\ref{prop:large} is not constructive. The table of
Section~\ref{sec:strategy} suggests a stronger statement.

\begin{conjecture}\label{conj:uniform}
The table strategy wins the square achievement game on $\Board_n$ for
every $n \ge 6$.
\end{conjecture}

The conjecture holds for $6 \le n \le 14$ by
Section~\ref{sec:verify}. The intended proof for general $n$ is a
saturation argument: the strategy's own stones stay in the corner
region, and the geometry bounds the reach of the opponent's play. The
elementary confinement lemma is already formalized.

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:span}
Let $x \ne y$ be distinct corners of a square $\Sq(r,c,s)$ with
$x, y \in \Board_B$. Then $r + s < 2B$ and $c + s < 2B$; in particular
$\Sq(r,c,s) \subseteq \Board_{2B}$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Two distinct corners either share a coordinate and differ by $s$ in
the other, or differ by $s$ in both; either way $s < B$. Each anchor
coordinate is bounded by the corresponding coordinate of a corner, so
$r, c < B$ and the claim follows.
\end{proof}

Consequently only boundedly many squares can ever pass through two
stones of the strategy's region, and a far reply interacts with play
only through its alignment class. What remains is the bookkeeping that
turns this confinement into a simulation between the game on
$\Board_n$ and the game on a fixed $\Board_{N_0}$; this is under way
in the formal development. One subtlety deserves record: the
opponent's forced blocks accumulate, and an aligned far stone can seed
squares whose side grows with the board, so class representatives must
be generated on boards large enough to exhibit the longest such
exchanges. A table generated on too small a board verifies there and
fails on larger boards; the table published here was generated on
$\Board_{15}$ after an earlier candidate, generated on $\Board_{12}$,
failed on $\Board_{13}$ in exactly this way.

\section{Reproducibility}\label{sec:repro}

The supplementary material contains the Lean development, with the
